# Moisés Saman

## Artist profile

**Selected Work:** [*Tripoli, Libya*](../works/04-moises-saman-44.md), 2011

**Sources:** [S13](../sources/source-register.md), [S26–S29](../sources/source-register.md), [S51](../sources/source-register.md)

Moisés Saman’s practice developed from conflict reporting into longer
photographic inquiries about memory, access, and the authority of the caption.
The token metadata uses the unaccented form `Moises Saman`; this profile follows
the artist’s accented name while retaining the issuer’s spelling in the source
record.

## From newspaper reporting to sustained inquiry

World Press Photo records Saman’s birth in Lima in 1974, his upbringing in
Barcelona, his studies in communications and sociology at California State
University, and his early interest in photography through images of the Balkan
wars. After a newspaper internship in California and a month photographing
postwar Kosovo, he joined *Newsday* as a staff photographer in 2000. His work
there included the aftermath of September 11 and sustained reporting in
Afghanistan, Iraq, and the wider Middle East.[^1]

Saman became a freelance photographer in 2007 and contributed to *The New York
Times*, Human Rights Watch, *Newsweek*, and *TIME*. World Press Photo records
his arrival at Magnum in 2010; Magnum’s fine-print biography identifies him as
a full member from 2014.[^2] The dates describe different stages of his
association with the agency.

His practice has increasingly examined how news events become longer histories.
The move from assignment to sequence allows uncertainty, repetition, and the
limits of caption language to remain visible. Walls, rooms, clothing, roads,
and damaged surfaces carry political weight without resolving every account
attached to them. A caption can name an event; the photograph can preserve a
visible trace; the two records answer different questions.

## Tripoli and controlled access

Saman’s Libya work belongs to a period when access, permission, and narrative
authority were inseparable from reporting. A retained TIME account describes
his 2011 assignment in Tripoli, confinement of foreign reporters at the Rixos
Hotel, and movement under government-minder supervision. The Committee to
Protect Journalists records the wider pattern of restricted movement,
monitoring, and pressure on the press.[^3]

These sources establish the conditions surrounding foreign reporting in
Tripoli. They do not identify the house in the selected photograph, the person
shown there, or the cause of the wall marks. Their importance lies in the route
through which a scene entered the camera and then the public record. The
caption’s qualification matters because the reporting environment was itself
part of the evidence.

## *Discordia* and the photographic sequence

The WARM Foundation describes *Discordia* as a long-form project developed
from 2011 to 2014 across sites of the Arab Spring. Its sequences move away from
a linear news structure and allow photographs to accumulate without captions.
The project was exhibited at WARM Festival in Sarajevo in 2015 and later at
the Bayeux Festival of War Correspondents; its 2016 self-published book
contains 126 photographs and three collages.[^4]

That structure provides a context for *Tripoli, Libya*. The selected
photograph’s visible facts are immediate: a marked wall, a caged lamp, a scarf,
a lowered head, and a figure of apparent youth. Its historical meaning arrives
through a caption and an access history. Saman’s wider practice makes that
relationship legible, while the reviewed public record does not establish that
this exact frame appears in *Discordia* or one of its exhibitions.

Later recognition, including Saman’s work with *The New Yorker* recorded by
the Pulitzer organization, belongs to a subsequent phase of that practice.[^5]
It supplies professional context without retrospectively verifying the 2011
Libya caption.

## The selected photograph

An apparently young person stands close to a pale wall. The wall is interrupted
by numerous dark marks and broken areas. A caged lamp hangs above. The figure
wears a dark coat and a light scarf; the head and gaze turn downward. The room
is shallow and frontal, with the wall occupying most of the frame.

The image gives the viewer a damaged surface and a vulnerable-looking
presence. It gives no name, exact age, biography, nationality, family
relationship, or confirmed location beyond the public title and the issuer’s
description of Tripoli. The marks are visible. Their cause remains outside the
visible evidence.

The issuer’s caption makes two separate statements. It records Libyan
government officials’ claim that Western air strikes targeted the house,
damaged it, and injured a woman. The same caption says that this claim was
impossible to verify. A separate passage reports that government minders
showed journalists evidence of strikes targeting civilian areas.[^6] The
second statement concerns what the minders presented; it does not verify the
first account or establish the cause of the marks in this image.

The photograph’s formal pressure comes from the distance between a visible
trace and the account attached to it. The wall occupies the space where proof
seems available, yet the image cannot supply a chain from mark to weapon, from
damage to responsibility, or from the reported injury to the depicted person.
The caged lamp gives the room a fixed point; the lowered head keeps the
person’s inner state unavailable. Saman’s image allows the claim to remain
present as a claim, with its uncertainty intact.

## Safeguarding and publication

The subject’s apparent youth requires a restrained public description. The
catalogue describes an apparently young person and retains the issuer’s word
“girl” only as attributed caption language. It assigns no exact age, name,
identity, nationality, family relationship, victim status, or witness status.
The reviewed public record supplies no image-specific identity, age, consent
documentation, precise house location, or later-life account.

Any public presentation should keep the caption’s qualification beside the
image. Interpretation should explain the government-managed access surrounding
the report without turning the officials’ account into fact. Alt text and
public metadata remain at visible-fact level, with no identifying or sensitive
location detail. Full-resolution presentation, crops, derivatives, or
preservation copies require image-specific rights authority and a safeguarding
decision that accounts for the subject’s apparent youth. The token’s `1 of 1`
designation and public Arweave locator supply neither.

Within the donor-formed group, Saman’s token is the sole selected work marked
curation `1/3`; the other four are marked `2/3`.[^6] Its contribution is
formal, historical, and ethical: a damaged surface offered as evidence, a
caption that qualifies its own causal account, a managed route into the room,
and a person whose public identity remains protected.
