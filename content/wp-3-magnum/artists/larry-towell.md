# Larry Towell

## Artist profile

**Work in this proposal:** [*Government soldiers in a church, Suchitoto, El Salvador*](../works/02-larry-towell-145.md), 1986
**Sources:** [S20–S21](../sources/source-register.md), [S33](../sources/source-register.md)

Larry Towell’s photography grew from long-form, self-directed documentary work
and a sustained engagement with Central America. Magnum’s public profile
describes his studies at York University, travel and volunteer work, his
photographs of the Nicaraguan Contra war and people disappeared in Guatemala,
and his 1988 membership in Magnum Photos.[^1] The profile also places his
practice in relation to landlessness, books, exhibitions, and institutional
collections. These strands matter because Towell’s photographs are not simply
assignments about conflict; they are a body of work built through duration,
return, and attention to lives shaped by displacement.

Towell’s Central American pictures occupy an important place in the history of
late twentieth-century documentary photography. Nicaragua, El Salvador, and
Guatemala appear not as interchangeable sites of violence but as political
landscapes in which military power, rural life, religious institutions, and
dispossession intersect. His work follows people and places over time, giving
the viewer a sense of conflict’s social geography rather than only its most
dramatic incident.

## The Central America projects

Towell’s *Gifts of War* exhibition, organized by the Art Gallery of Windsor and
shown across Canadian venues from 1988 to 1989, placed photographs and
testimonies from Nicaragua, El Salvador, and Guatemala within a human-rights
framework.[^2] His book *El Salvador* followed in 1997, extending the country’s
war and aftermath into a sustained photobook form. The exhibition and book
records establish the breadth and duration of the work while leaving room for
object-level research: a catalogue title does not by itself identify every
frame, page, or negative.

An institutional parallel at Art Windsor-Essex records a gelatin silver print
from an El Salvador series titled *Soldiers in Church, Suchitoto, El Salvador*.
The record gives a 1986–1987 series context, physical dimensions, and a
collection history.[^3] It is valuable evidence of how Towell’s work entered a
museum, and a useful lead for comparing manifestations. The public record does
not publish the Magnum archive number for the proposed token image, so the
parallel remains a research relation rather than a completed equivalence.

## Form, witness, and distance

Towell’s photographs often balance direct witness with carefully organized
space. Interiors, roads, fields, and bodies become structures through which
viewers can sense the distribution of power. The composition is never a neutral
container. Its symmetry, intervals, and points of attention determine how a
viewer meets people who may be vulnerable, armed, displaced, or watched.

That formal intelligence creates an ethical question. A beautiful composition
can make violence legible, but it can also make violence seem settled and
consumable. Towell’s long-term approach keeps the social world around the
incident in view. The work asks the viewer to remain with the place and with the
limits of a single encounter.

The Salvadoran context is central to that practice. Official peace and
truth-commission records describe the civil war, church social action,
repression, and the eventual peace process.[^4] Such histories give the church
image a serious political setting. They also make precise cataloguing more
important: a general history of church persecution should not be converted into
an assertion about an unnamed building or soldier.
