## Label

*CENTURY #401* removes the strong color contrasts seen elsewhere in the project and makes cutting an architecture of tone. A large gray circular field is interrupted by heavy black columns, translucent overlaps, and ten vertical slices. Fifteen white lines cross the square diagonally, passing through the darker planes like taut wires. Near the lower right, a transparent oval seems both embedded in the disk and suspended before it.

Reas’s documented controls permit the work to be cut again or returned to its original order, while the project description identifies the lines as moving.[^2] The Museum’s retained observation did not activate those controls, so no particular restored image is claimed here.[^3] Reas adapted *CENTURY*’s procedure from Ellsworth Kelly’s method of dividing and recombining compositions. He began the wider body in 2012; this exact Art Blocks work was released in 2021.[^2] In the Museum’s three-work group, #401 is the work in which recombination most clearly crosses from collage into architecture.

## Extended entry

The square of *CENTURY #401* is gray, black, and white, with a layered and oddly tactile tonal range. A large gray disk fills most of the field. Heavy black uprights interrupt it, especially at the left, while narrower columns establish a broken vertical cadence across the image. Fifteen white lines pull in other directions. They cross diagonally, sometimes nearly parallel and sometimes at acute angles, as though stretched across the face of a structure. Near the lower right, a darker translucent oval overlaps the disk. Its edge can be followed until a black plane or slice seam interrupts it, then recovered elsewhere.

Color does not separate the elements; opacity does. Solid black columns stop the gaze. The gray disk receives it. The translucent oval allows one tone to pass through another, producing a third condition that belongs fully to neither. That modulation gives #401 a depth absent from a simple arrangement of black and white shapes. The image alternates between surface and shallow construction: one moment a cut paper collage, the next a bank of partitions with an elliptical chamber behind them. The white lines complicate both readings because they appear to run continuously across planes that otherwise conceal one another.

Ten vertical slices give this unstable architecture a measured, structural rhythm between #31's close divisions and #724's broad ones. Each slice carries part of the original composition to a new position. A black rim becomes a freestanding column; the translucent oval becomes an aperture; sections of the gray circle become alternating walls. Recombination changes categories as well as positions: edge becomes object, overlap becomes room, and disk becomes façade.

The official still preserves one especially resolved cut state, while the artist’s documentation explains the operation that constructs it.[^2] The black uprights and displaced sections are the visible consequences of a program that can alter the slice order; the documented restoration control makes an original order available without licensing an unsupported description of what that restored image looks like. Reas also specifies that the fine lines move, adding a second duration to the sudden event of recombination: a continuous shift against heavier forms. The Museum’s retained two-frame observation supports change over time, but because the screenshot bytes were not retained and no controls were used, this account does not claim to reconstruct either frame or a witnessed transition.[^3]

*CENTURY* treats slicing as an active operation. Reas developed the project from a procedure associated with Ellsworth Kelly, who cut compositions into vertical columns and rearranged them.[^2] In #401, the historical reference appears as an operation. The work begins with an abstract composition, partitions it, and makes the partition active. Executable software changes the terms: a material collage preserves the edges of a past cut in a final physical order, while #401 keeps the underlying arrangement recoverable, permits further cuts, and specifies continuing line movement after a recombination.

#401 therefore shifts attention from the finished arrangement to the persistence of relations across states. The gray disk remains conceptually present even when its parts read as walls. The translucent ellipse remains an overlap even when the cut turns it into a sequence of windows. The white lines continue their trajectories despite the apparent depth of the black columns. The eye reconstructs what the program has separated, then abandons that reconstruction when the same fragments cohere as a new space. The work does not ask which state is correct. It makes the act of moving between readings its subject.

Its medium is essential to that movement. Code establishes drawing, slicing, recombination, motion, and interaction. Token-derived data gives #401 its grayscale palette, number of lines and slices, transparent layer, and initial configuration. The browser executes those relations; the screen makes their different tempos visible. The ERC-721 token records chain identity and transfer history; legal title, copyright, and Museum accession are recorded separately. A still documents the exact tonal organization of one state; the live manifestation shows columns collapsing back into a disk and lines travelling across it.

The 6529 Network Museum holds three *CENTURY* works as a comparative group, not as an artist-designated triptych. Within that group, #401 occupies the tonal and architectural position. #31 uses blue, cream, and close slicing to build density; #724 uses cream, rust, and broad intervals to make displacement legible across emptiness. #401 shows how the same procedure can create depth without hue. Its transparent element is especially important: it introduces a mode of partial concealment between the project’s more emphatic acts of covering and exposing.

The result is an abstract work that never settles for flatness, although nothing in it leaves the plane of the display. #401’s black columns are made from displaced parts of a circle; its recesses are differences in opacity; its wires are moving lines. Architecture occurs as a perceptual consequence of cutting. By allowing that construction to be undone and remade, Reas turns the square into both image and instrument—a place where a disk can repeatedly become a room.
