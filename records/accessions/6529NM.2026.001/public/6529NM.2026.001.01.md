## Label

*CENTURY #31* first appears as an image under pressure. A nearly full circular field has been cut into narrow upright sections: saturated blue wedges, dark slate columns, and cream-colored arcs no longer meet where the eye expects. Fine cream and salmon lines cross these heavier forms without restoring their continuity. Reas’s project description identifies those lines as moving and provides controls for a new slicing operation or a return to the original order. These documented possibilities disclose why the still should be read as one state of an executable work, not as its terminal image.[^2]

Reas began the wider *CENTURY* body in 2012 and developed its central operation from an approach associated with Ellsworth Kelly—making a composition, cutting it into columns, and rearranging those columns. The exact Art Blocks work was released in 2021, translating that procedure into software that remains active.[^2] Among the Museum’s three *CENTURY* works, #31 is the most compressed. Its sixteen slices turn a lucid abstract image into a contest between monument and seam: the picture survives, but only as something repeatedly interrupted.

## Extended entry

The first encounter with *CENTURY #31* is frontal and congested. A circular field occupies almost the entire square, but the circle cannot gather itself into a single figure. It has been divided into vertical strips whose edges behave like faults. Saturated blues rise in wedges and columns; dark slate passages block them; cream-colored forms appear as partial eyes, moons, or apertures, only to disappear behind another band. Seventeen fine lines—mostly cream, with salmon accents—cross the heavier geometry at diagonals. They seem at first to promise connection, yet their oblique trajectories make the interruption more acute. The large shapes belong to an image that has been broken; the lines belong to a space that continues across the break.

That distinction between mass and line gives #31 its particular force. The blue carries different weights: a broad plane here, a narrow upright there, a wedge caught behind cream. The cream forms are equally unstable. Some read as circular bodies; others narrow to curved edges, as if the greater part of an oculus were concealed. Because the sixteen cuts are close together, the composition has little empty space in which to recover. Its vertical rhythm is insistent—more like a bank of shutters or architectural piers than a collage laid flat. Reas’s slicing operation scrambles the image and gives the screen a shallow, contradictory depth: one section appears to stand before the next even though every mark lies on the same plane.

The live work exceeds the official still, although the Museum’s retained observation establishes only that two frames captured after a controlled wait had different hashes; no keyboard controls were activated during that session.[^3] Reas’s own project description identifies the fine lines as moving and specifies that the audience can cut and reassemble the composition with one command and restore its original order with another.[^2] Those capabilities prevent the diagonal network from becoming merely an ornamental overlay: each line is potentially an event passing through a structure that cannot wholly contain it, while the existing vertical congestion remains one arrangement among others. What looks in the still like the work’s native architecture is therefore better understood as an archive of cuts imposed upon an underlying image—an interpretation grounded in the documented operation, not in a claimed Museum witnessing of the restoration.

Reas has described *CENTURY* as an homage to abstraction, and its central operation derives specifically from Kelly’s practice of cutting compositions into columns and recombining them.[^2] The relation is procedural. #31 takes an act once performed with painted paper and makes it a repeatable rule. Software changes the terms of that rule. A physical recombination culminates in a chosen arrangement. Here, the base composition, the current cut state, further recombinations, and continuous line movement coexist as documented possibilities of one token-bound work. The cut becomes a condition through which the image keeps passing.

*CENTURY #31* resides across linked layers. Source code defines its possibilities; token-derived state supplies this blue, cream, and slate configuration; browser execution produces movement and interaction; the Ethereum token records chain identity and transfer history; the screen makes the process visible. Legal title, copyright, and Museum accession are recorded separately. The official PNG preserves an indispensable documentary view of one state within a work that remains open to another cut.

Within the 6529 Network Museum’s three-work *CENTURY* group, #31 establishes the pole of density and occlusion. #724 demonstrates how the same project can make open cream space and a few displaced arcs carry the composition; #401 replaces strong color contrast with grayscale overlap and a more explicitly architectural depth. #31 crowds the field until almost every passage participates in concealment. That comparison matters because the three works were not conceived by the artist as a triptych. Their value together lies in demonstrating how one procedure can produce meaningfully different spatial arguments without treating difference as a hierarchy.

The work’s title places this procedure within a long century of abstraction, but #31 refuses the familiar fantasy of abstract composition as timeless equilibrium. Its image is balanced only provisionally. The vertical slices preserve evidence of a prior arrangement, the moving lines submit the present to duration, and the controls hold another reconfiguration in reserve. What appears monumental is therefore also contingent. #31’s achievement is to make that contingency visible without dissolving into spectacle: a heavy blue-and-cream construction, carrying its own interruption as part of its form.
