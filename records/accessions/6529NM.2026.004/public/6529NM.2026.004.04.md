# Village of Maronal, Vale do Javari Indigenous Territory, Amazonas

**Artist:** Sebastião Salgado (1944–2025)  
**Date:** 2018  
**Project:** Amazônia

**Source image:** 3543 × 5315 pixels  
**Edition identifier:** Token 2216  
**Museum identifier:** 6529NM.2026.004.04

## Label

A young sitter looks toward the camera, the head turned slightly within a
vertical frame. Pale strands cross the forehead and descend beside the face.
A dark painted band connects the eyes; smaller marks appear on the cheeks.
The plain background concentrates attention on the face and the different
directions of hair, ornament and painted lines.

The source caption locates this portrait in the Marubo village of Maronal,
in the Vale do Javari. It does not identify the sitter by name. The
photograph's visual directness gives a strong sense of encounter, while the
caption supplies only part of its personal and social setting.[^1]

## A face within a vertical frame

The portrait's scale and orientation distinguish it from the other five
photographs in the Museum group. The sitter occupies most of a tall frame.
Long strands run down both sides of the face and continue beyond the lower
edge. Their pale surfaces remain distinct from the darker hair. Across the
forehead, similar strands take a nearly horizontal direction, making a wide
band above the eyes.

The painted marks give the face a different order. A dark band passes around
the eyes and across the bridge of the nose. Small groups of dark dots sit on
the cheeks, while a larger painted area crosses the lower face. These marks
are visible in considerable detail, with differences in density and edge
that give each area a distinct relation to the light.

The head turns slightly while the eyes remain directed toward the camera.
This small difference matters: the portrait has a frontal encounter without
the strict symmetry of a fully frontal pose. Light picks out the nearer cheek
and nose, and the pale strands frame the face unevenly. Stray hairs cross
some of the more ordered surfaces, introducing fine interruptions into the
arrangement.

The dark background removes most indications of immediate surroundings. In
a 2022 interview, Salgado described carrying a substantial portable studio
backdrop on his Amazon expeditions.[^2] The plain field gives this portrait
an organized space of encounter, concentrating the relation between sitter,
camera and the light falling across the face.

The location gives the portrait a social setting beyond the frame. UNIVAJA's
Marubo account lists Maronal among the Curuçá villages and describes bodily
ornament as sacred. It also records the people's own designation, Yora, and
their continuing work for territorial and cultural autonomy.[^3] These
statements place the pictured ornament within a community's account of its
own practices and history.

UNIVAJA's institutional history adds another relevant scale. Its account
follows Marubo leadership within a wider movement for health, education,
territorial recognition and political representation.[^4] That history makes
it difficult to treat the portrait as an image of a society outside historical
change. The quietness of the photographic background is a condition of this
picture, not evidence of isolation from a contemporary public world.

The absence of a personal name remains significant. It prevents the catalogue
from carrying the specificity of the face into a biographical account. The
image nevertheless gives the viewer many particular things to examine:
the angle of the head, the relation of the gaze to the camera, the different
densities of the painted surfaces, and the arrangement of strands around the
face. These details sustain the encounter even where the personal account
remains incomplete.

Within the six-work group, this portrait changes the viewer's distance from
the subject most sharply. The landscape images allow the eye to range over
extensive terrain. Here, attention is returned by a gaze. That change also
places a limit on the group's environmental argument: the sitter is a person
in a particular community, with a history extending beyond the role this
photograph occupies in Salgado's project or the Museum's sequence.
