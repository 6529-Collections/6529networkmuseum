# Amazônia: photographs, books and a digital edition

**Project study** · 6529 Network Museum · Version 0.1.1 · 13 September 2026  
**Editorial status:** review pending

The photographs in *Amazônia* move between expanses of forest and encounters
with people. Their differences in scale are substantial. A ridge can
occupy much of a horizon; a painted band can cross the width of a face.
Both are made legible through the placement of light and dark forms, but
they offer different kinds of access to their subjects. A project that
holds them together must also account for the distance between them.

Salgado devoted six years to the Amazon project presented in the 2021 book
and exhibition. The publisher describes work with twelve Indigenous
communities. Lélia Wanick Salgado designed the book and curated the exhibition,
whose installation at MAXXI ran from October 2021 to August 2022.[^1]
The project therefore reached audiences through edited sequences and
physical arrangements as well as individual photographs. The space between
images was part of its presentation.

In a 2022 interview, Salgado described choosing to photograph intact forest
as an argument for its protection. The expeditions required substantial
logistical support. His account also describes digital cameras and duplicate
memory cards.[^2] The photographs' apparent immediacy was the result of work
carried out over time, with the transport, equipment and relationships needed
to make repeated visits possible.

The six source JPEGs now held for the Museum's gift bear the marks of
digital production. All contain an Adobe RGB profile; their EXIF fields
identify digital cameras and later image-processing software. These are
finished image files associated with the edition. They are distinct from
camera originals and from the printed manifestations produced for books
and exhibitions. Their precise dimensions and byte hashes give the Museum
a means of identifying the files it has examined.[^3]

## The 2022 edition

Sotheby's announced an *Amazônia* blind mint of 5,000 photographs for
12 October 2022. The same digital catalogue discussed *Tree of Life*, a
separate film offered at auction. Those offerings belong to the same
period of Salgado's work with digital editions, but the Museum's six objects
are photographic tokens from the *Amazônia* contract.[^4]

The edition gives each token a number and a metadata record. It does not
supply the Museum with an artist-authored sequence of these six images.
Their metadata uses recurring categories, including forest, aerial views
and community names. These fields help organize an extensive body of
photographs, while the image entries recover more particular distinctions:
densely overlapping vegetation, a layered mountain view, weather over forest, a
portrait, a winding river and a gathering at Nova Esperança.

The issuer's records also contain broad environmental and historical
descriptions. Some are shared across photographs. Their function differs
from a caption identifying an exact place or person. In the river view,
for example, the metadata identifies Roraima but leaves the watercourse
unnamed. The photograph remains a specific image even where its written
identification is less precise.

The Museum has assembled *Amazônia: Forest, Water, and Community* as a
six-work group within this larger edition. Its sequence moves through
different scales of attention. The close forest view is followed by
mountain and atmospheric views; the portrait interrupts that distance;
the river restores an elevated view before the final community scene
returns to people within a place. This order is one way of reading the
works together. Individual entries allow the photographs to be encountered
separately as well.

## A photographic object on a network

An *Amazônia* token identifies an object whose image is retrieved separately.
At the Museum's recorded Ethereum state, each of the six token URIs led
to metadata on an Arweave gateway, and each metadata record named a JPEG
through another gateway URL.[^3] The visible photograph depends on that
relationship being maintained or recoverable.

This structure makes the caption and source file part of the object's
study. The token number alone cannot disclose the river's bends or the
portrait's orientation; the JPEG alone does not record the transfer to
the Museum. Together, the image, metadata and transaction history
document this manifestation's passage from the edition into a public
collection. The earlier book and exhibition remain essential contexts,
without making every digital image equivalent to a particular print or
installation photograph.

For display, the Museum preserves the complete frame and the relationship
between its tones. Responsive copies permit different screen sizes without
substituting a crop for the work. The six photographs share a visual
language, but their proportions and internal rhythms differ. Giving the
portrait its full vertical extent and the river its uninterrupted course
allows those differences to remain part of the encounter.
