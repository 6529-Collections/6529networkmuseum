# River through forest, Roraima

**Artist:** Sebastião Salgado (1944–2025)  
**Date:** 2018  
**Project:** Amazônia  
**Medium:** Black-and-white digital photograph; JPEG associated with an ERC-721 token  
**Source image:** 3543 × 2362 pixels  
**Edition identifier:** Token 2768  
**Museum identifier:** 6529NM.2026.004.05  
**Credit:** Gift of punk6529. Photograph © Sebastião Salgado.  
**Text:** 6529 Network Museum, version 1.0.0, 13 September 2026; scholarship reviewed

## Label

A pale river curves through dark forest, broad in the foreground and narrow
in the distance. Its bends carry the eye across the photograph in stages.
Where two stretches of water approach one another, a strip of trees keeps
them apart; farther away, the course becomes difficult to follow.

The elevated viewpoint makes a large area visible while withholding the
experience of travelling through it. There is no sky or open horizon. The
forest occupies the whole frame around the water, and the river supplies
its strongest differences in shape and tone. The issuer places the photograph
in Roraima without naming the watercourse. Within the Museum's six-work group,
it gives water a continuous form, dividing the broad canopy into areas
that differ from the closer Jaú photograph's crowded vegetation.[^1]

## Following the bends

The river enters the frame at the lower right. Its light surface is wide
enough here to be read as an area, with a distinct inner and outer bank.
Farther into the picture, the same surface contracts into a line. This
change makes distance visible through width: what appears accessible in
the foreground becomes a sequence of small turns toward the upper left.

Those turns prevent a direct passage across the image. Following the
channel means looking alternately left and right, sometimes doubling back
to distinguish one stretch of water from the next. A loop on the right
nearly closes around a tongue of forest. Elsewhere, narrow bands of trees
separate reaches that appear close together from this height. The water's
continuity is easier to understand than to trace without interruption.

The forest has a different visual rhythm. Small variations in the canopy
recur across a much larger dark field. They register individual crowns and
uneven surfaces, yet seldom interrupt the river's dominance. The apparent
scale of the view comes from the relation between these two kinds of detail:
the channel changes direction conspicuously, while the vegetation repeats
many smaller differences around it.

Salgado excludes the sky. Distance must therefore be found within the
ground, through the diminishing channel and the changing clarity of the
trees. The image offers considerable reach but no place outside the forest
from which to measure its extent. Even the river continues beyond the frame.
The photograph's edges cut a portion from a system that they do not contain.

The comparison with the aerial cloud photograph is useful. There, a low
horizon separates land from weather, and broad shadows mark the passage
of clouds over the canopy. Here, the channel organizes almost everything
visible. Seen together, the two photographs distinguish water gathered in
a course from water suspended or falling through the atmosphere. The
comparison arises from their forms; it does not require treating either
image as a diagram of the whole Amazon basin.

The issuer's category, `AERIAL VIEWS`, accurately describes the vantage.
Its geographical field supplies the state of Roraima.[^1] The photograph
itself contributes the particular course of the river: its repeated turns,
the changing breadth of its surface and the forest held between its bends.
These features give the work a specificity that the broad category cannot.
