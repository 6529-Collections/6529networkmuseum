# Forest, Water, and Community


The six photographs in *Amazônia: Forest, Water, and Community* bring together
landscapes and portraits from Sebastião Salgado's Amazon project. Made between
2016 and 2019, they show forest beside the Jaú River, the Imeri mountains,
weather and a river in Roraima, and people in Marubo and Yawanawá communities.
The Museum formed this selection from the 2022 digital edition. Punk6529
donated the six works in 2026.[^1]

## Amazônia and the environmental project

Salgado made the beauty and abundance of the Amazon central to his argument
for its protection. In a 2022 interview, he described concentrating on intact
forest and the Indigenous communities living within it. He wanted the
photographs to encourage a public commitment to their future.[^2]

That purpose helps explain the expansive character of these landscapes.
Salgado gives the forest an imposing presence: mountains rise above a
wooded plain, rivers extend through the canopy, and clouds gather over it.

## Forest, water and weather

In the Jaú photograph, palm trunks and fronds fill
the frame, their light surfaces emerging from deep shade. In the aerial
photographs, the canopy becomes a continuous expanse. A river's repeated
bends give one Roraima view its shape; in the other, cloud shadows spread
across the forest while rain falls in the distance.

Water connects these subjects. The exhibition's account of *Amazônia*
places rivers and weather within the forest's water cycle, including
the movement of moisture from vegetation into the atmosphere.[^3]
Seen together, the photographs give this relationship a physical presence:
water moving through the land, rain descending toward it, and vegetation
covering the ground.

## People and place

The portrait made at Maronal concentrates on a sitter's face and gaze.
At Nova Esperança, Salgado works within a wider scene: a figure with a
feather headdress stands close to the camera, with other people gathered
behind.[^4]

Lélia Wanick Salgado's exhibition design made the relationship between
landscape and community central to the visitor's experience. At the
Science Museum, suspended landscape photographs surrounded structures
evoking Indigenous homes. Portraits and scenes of daily life were accompanied
by films in which Indigenous leaders spoke about their territories.[^5]
