## The gift

An armed figure looks across a desert border outside the frame. Two soldiers stand in a church beneath the crucifix. A runner crosses a cloud of tear gas at the Western Wall. A child stands before damage whose cause reporters could not verify. A soldier sits among the remains of a temple destroyed months before the photograph was made. Combat itself remains largely outside these frames. The group attends to the borders, institutions, captions, and aftermath through which violence acquires meaning.

Made between 1952 and 2016 by David Seymour, Larry Towell, Micha Bar-Am, Moisés Saman, and Lorenzo Meloni, the five photographs locate authority in a border, a church, a sacred enclosure, a managed press visit, and an archaeological ruin. Four were selected for Azu Nwagbogu's second Magnum 75 curation, organized around the “indecisive moment.” Saman's photograph comes from Alejandro Cartagena's first curation and makes uncertainty explicit: the government presented a damaged house as evidence, while the accompanying caption withheld verification.

The donor formed the group from five individual works. Magnum, the five photographers, and the two original curators have made no claim for it as a series. The group title is the donor's proposal and does not attribute the grouping to any of them.

## Magnum Photos

[Magnum Photos](https://store.magnumphotos.com/pages/magnum-photos) was formed in 1947 by a group that included Robert Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson, George Rodger, David “Chim” Seymour, and William Vandivert. Organized as a cooperative owned and operated by its member photographers, Magnum gave photographers an unusual degree of authority over their work at a time when illustrated magazines and agencies commonly controlled assignments, negatives, and publication. Its membership remains self-selecting; only about one hundred photographers have become full members since its founding.

The cooperative became one of the central institutions of postwar documentary photography. Its archive joins news assignments to long-form essays, books, exhibitions, and bodies of work that continued to acquire meaning after their first publication. Magnum has never represented a single visual style or political position. Its historical importance lies partly in the sustained tension between evidence and authorship: its photographers report events while making images shaped by individual judgment, access, form, and experience.

The five photographers in this proposal span that history. Seymour was a founder. Towell and Bar-Am developed long records of work in places where they were also personally or politically implicated. Saman and Meloni belong to a later generation that has examined the conditions under which conflict is reported, narrated, and remembered.

## Magnum Photos 75

In 2022, its seventy-fifth anniversary year, Magnum announced three planned NFT collections drawn from its historical and expanding archive. The first public release page listed seventy-six images selected by photographer and Obscura co-founder Alejandro Cartagena and offered in six drops from 28 June to 12 July. Cartagena approached the archive as an evolving photographic language, choosing images for the formal and narrative qualities that had allowed them to endure.

A second release of seventy-five images was selected in dialogue with Azu Nwagbogu, founder and director of the African Artists’ Foundation. Nwagbogu placed his selection against the familiar idea of Henri Cartier-Bresson’s “decisive moment.” His “indecisive moment” concerned photographs charged with expectation and unresolved meaning—images that invite renewed scrutiny rather than settling the events they show. Four works in this gift came through that second selection. Saman’s photograph, from Cartagena’s first selection, makes the problem of evidence explicit and gives the group an important point of comparison.

The project brought photographs made between the 1940s and the present into a new system of editioning and circulation. Each token was described as a 1/1 NFT and linked to an image and metadata on Arweave. That designation belongs to the token edition; it does not make the underlying photograph unique, supersede its negative, print, and publication histories, or transfer copyright.

Magnum announced approximately 225 works across three releases. The evidence reviewed here establishes the first and second selections and 148 live tokens at the proposal’s finalized observation block. It does not establish that all announced works were minted or that a third selection was released. The gap remains explicit in this record.

# David Seymour

_Patrolling the border between the Negev Desert and Jordan_, 1952

Magnum Photos 75 #127 · 1/1 ERC-721 · Negev, Israel

© David Seymour/Magnum Photos 2022. All Rights Reserved.

Seymour builds the photograph around a line that cannot be seen. An armed, back-facing figure fills the lower frame, his rifle rising diagonally toward a bright canvas shelter set deep in a valley of dark, stratified rock. His body supplies scale and point of view: the landscape is encountered from a guarded position. No marker in the image identifies the armistice line or the person watching it. Border and patrol come from the caption; the photograph gives their spatial pressure.

The David Seymour estate publishes a visually matching frame and locates it in the King Solomon's Mines area on the Negev–Jordan border. Its public record does not cross-index the estate filename to the Magnum archive number. In 1952 this was the unsettled armistice demarcation created by the 1949 Israel–Jordan agreement.

## The photographer

[David Seymour](https://store.magnumphotos.com/collections/david-seymour), born Dawid Szymin in Warsaw in 1911 and known professionally as Chim, began photographing for the left-wing French press in the 1930s. He covered the Popular Front and the Spanish Civil War, but his historical standing rests as much on what followed combat as on the battlefield itself. His postwar commissions for UNESCO and UNICEF traced displacement, injury, and the lives of European children after the Second World War. The International Center of Photography has described his approach as humanistic: political catastrophe entered his pictures through the people living with its consequences.

Seymour co-founded Magnum in 1947 and became its president after Robert Capa’s death in 1954. He photographed the establishment of Israel, European political and cultural life, and postwar reconstruction before being killed in 1956 while covering a prisoner exchange during the Suez Crisis. The Negev photograph sits within that postwar engagement with Israel and contested territory. Within this group, it brings Magnum’s founding generation into view through an exact archival object whose commissioner, first publication, assignment dates, and foreground figure remain unestablished.

# Larry Towell

_Government soldiers in a church, Suchitoto, El Salvador_, 1986

Magnum Photos 75 #145 · 1/1 ERC-721 · Suchitoto, El Salvador

© Larry Towell/Magnum Photos 2022. All Rights Reserved.

Two soldiers face the camera in the lower half of a nearly symmetrical church interior. Their rifles angle away from their bodies as the crucifix rises between and above them. Windows admit light from both sides; the altar flowers are small and low. Towell sets armed presence against sacred architecture, giving each order—military, religious, pictorial—an axis of its own.

The source caption places the church near the Guazapa guerrilla stronghold and connects the scene to persecution of the Catholic Church during El Salvador's civil war. That context does not identify the church, the soldiers, or their purpose there. Their identities and unit, and the image's initial assignment and publication, remain unestablished in the public sources reviewed here.

Within this proposed group, the photograph is the most frontal: two soldiers face the camera, and the church's symmetry holds their presence in place. The image leaves the scene's precise circumstances unresolved.

## The photographer

[Larry Towell](https://store.magnumphotos.com/collections/larry-towell), born in rural Ontario in 1953, studied visual art before working as a writer, musician, and oral historian. His photographic practice took shape in Central America during the 1980s: he documented the Contra war in Nicaragua, families of the disappeared in Guatemala, and the civil war in El Salvador. He joined Magnum in 1988. The Suchitoto photograph therefore belongs to the period that formed his public practice, even though its exact assignment and first publication have not been recovered.

Towell’s later work has ranged across Mennonite communities in Canada and Mexico, his own family farm, Palestine, Afghanistan, and Standing Rock. The recurring subject is land: who possesses it, who is removed from it, and how displacement enters private life. He has often combined photographs with writing, recorded testimony, music, and bookmaking, allowing a conflict to be seen over time and beyond a single news event. He received the Henri Cartier-Bresson Award in 2003 and the Prix Nadar in 2005; his work is held by institutions including the National Gallery of Canada, the Getty, and the George Eastman Museum. In the proposed gift, the church interior is an early, concentrated statement of a question that continues throughout his work: what happens when armed power enters a community’s social and sacred ground.

# Micha Bar-Am

_Demonstration, Western Wall, Jerusalem_, 1989

Magnum Photos 75 #97 · 1/1 ERC-721 · Jerusalem

© Micha Bar-Am/Magnum Photos 2022. All Rights Reserved.

A figure runs from left to right through a field of smoke, one arm extended beyond the edge of the frame. The camera catches a canister in the air. The Western Wall enters as a band of masonry; metal menorahs and a low barrier cross the foreground. Sacred place, police action, and bodily urgency occupy the same shallow space.

The token caption associates the photograph with protests over women's collective prayer at the Western Wall. Contemporary legal, government, and news records document confrontation and police tear gas in March 1989. The precise day, the central person's identity, and any membership in Women of the Wall remain unresolved. “Western Wall” is used here as the current place name; “Wailing Wall” appears in some historical collection records.

The same frame appears in public records from the Micha Bar-Am Archive, the International Center of Photography, and Museum Wales. Together, those records support its identification across institutions while leaving the precise date and the central person's identity unresolved. Smoke makes the limit of visibility literal: the event is visible while person and moment remain open.

## The photographer

[Micha Bar-Am](https://www.icp.org/browse/archive/constituents/micha-bar-am) was born in Berlin in 1930 and moved with his family to Mandatory Palestine in 1936. After military service in 1948 and work on kibbutzim, he began photographing archaeological expeditions and communal life. He covered the 1956 Sinai War, joined the staff of the Israeli military magazine _Bamachane_, and photographed the Six-Day War in 1967. He became a Magnum correspondent in 1968 and worked for _The New York Times_ in Israel for more than two decades.

Bar-Am also helped Cornell Capa establish the International Center of Photography and, from 1977 to 1992, led the photography department at the Tel Aviv Museum of Art. His career therefore crosses roles often held apart: participant, military and newspaper photographer, independent author, and museum curator. His own account of documentary practice stresses the difficult distance between involvement and judgment, and between public evidence and personal vision. The 1989 Western Wall photograph belongs at the center of that tension. It emerges from a lifetime of photographing Israel and its neighbors, while its smoke, movement, and incomplete identification prevent the event from becoming a closed illustration.

# Moisés Saman

_Tripoli, Libya_, 2011

Magnum Photos 75 #44 · 1/1 ERC-721 · Tripoli, Libya

© Moisés Saman/Magnum Photos 2022. All Rights Reserved.

A child, described in the source caption as a girl, stands against a white wall covered by punctures and broken plaster. A caged lamp hangs above her. The light scarf across her dark coat lifts toward the right while her head and eyes turn down. The photograph places a living body beside marks offered as proof, yet it withholds any visual means of assigning those marks a cause.

Libyan government officials brought foreign journalists to the house and said Western air strikes had damaged it and injured a woman. Saman's caption states that the claim could not be verified. The cited accounts describe government-controlled access for foreign journalists in Tripoli. The exact house and date, the weapon or responsible force, the reported injury, and the identity of the child remain unresolved.

Within this proposed group, the photograph tests how a caption can preserve uncertainty while an official account seeks to turn visible damage into evidence. The image leaves the claim unresolved; the caption records its limits.

## The photographer

[Moisés Saman](https://www.worldpressphoto.org/moises-saman) was born in Lima in 1974, raised largely in Barcelona, and studied communications and sociology in California. After an early trip to photograph postwar Kosovo, he joined _Newsday_ in 2000. The aftermath of September 11 led to sustained work in Afghanistan, Iraq, and the wider Middle East. He joined Magnum in 2010 and became a full member in 2014.

Saman’s books have increasingly examined the photographer’s power within the histories he records. _Discordia_ (2016) rejected a simple chronology of the Arab uprisings; _Glad Tidings of Benevolence_ (2023) set twenty years of his Iraq photographs against military language, archival documents, and competing accounts of the war. In 2025 he received the Pulitzer Prize for Feature Photography for work on the legacy of Syria’s detention system. _Tripoli, Libya_ belongs to the formative Arab-uprising period of this practice. Its importance lies in the relation Saman establishes between picture and caption: he records the scene made available to him while refusing the government’s claim that the visible damage proves its cause.

# Lorenzo Meloni

_Palmyra, Syria_, 1 April 2016

Magnum Photos 75 #104 · 1/1 ERC-721 · Palmyra, Syria

© Lorenzo Meloni/Magnum Photos 2022. All Rights Reserved.

Pale stone and hard sunlight spread across a wide frame. A soldier sits low in the foreground rubble, lifting his helmet over his face. His camouflage and rifle mark the military present; broken columns and standing portico fragments carry a much longer history. The body is small beside the field of remains. A gesture that might suggest exhaustion, shielding, or adjustment stays unresolved.

UNESCO records that Islamic State destroyed the principal structure of the Temple of Bel in August 2015. Government and allied forces seized Palmyra from ISIL on 27 March 2016; Meloni photographed there five days later under Syrian government permission while moving with government forces. The photograph is situated in the aftermath of destruction and recapture. The Temple of Bel had been destroyed months earlier, while the standing structures complicate any account of ancient Palmyra as wholly lost.

As the latest-dated work, the photograph brings the group to a site where military action and cultural loss meet. Its high light keeps the damaged stone fully exposed instead of turning it into a theatrical ruin. In the photograph's historical setting, the site is under military control.

## The photographer

[Lorenzo Meloni](https://www.magnumphotos.com/photographer/lorenzo-meloni/), born in Rome in 1983, has spent more than a decade photographing conflict and its aftermath in the Middle East and North Africa. His long projects have followed Libya after the fall of Muammar Gaddafi, the wars in Syria and Iraq, the rise and territorial collapse of Islamic State, and the political histories through which those conflicts are narrated. He became a full member of Magnum in 2020.

Meloni describes his approach as historicist. A ruined city enters his pictures within older histories of empire, borders, monuments, and recurring destruction. His 2022 book _We Don’t Say Goodbye_ draws on a decade of work around Islamic State and the states, landscapes, and people altered by its rise and fall. The Palmyra photograph belongs squarely to this sustained field of inquiry. It does not show the Temple of Bel being destroyed. It shows a soldier and an ancient site after destruction and military recapture, when the ruin has already entered new political and photographic uses.

# Conflict at Its Edges

_Five Photographs of Evidence and Aftermath, 1952–2016_

## The case for selection

Combat itself remains largely outside these five photographs. Seymour’s patrol surveys a border absent from the image. Towell’s soldiers occupy a church. Bar-Am catches a figure moving through tear gas where worship, gender, and public authority meet. Saman photographs a child beside damage presented by officials as evidence and left unverified in the accompanying caption. Meloni encounters a soldier among the remains of Palmyra after destruction and military recapture.

Their shared subject is the way conflict becomes legible. An armistice line, a sanctuary under military occupation, a contested sacred enclosure, a government-managed press visit, and an archaeological ruin each determine what the photographer can see and what a viewer is asked to believe. Power enters the image through position, access, architecture, caption, and historical knowledge. Interpretation begins at the limits of the frame.

This oblique relation to the event gives the group its force. Documentary photography has often been organized around decisive action and visually consumable suffering. Here the event is dispersed across anticipation, occupation, confrontation, official claim, and aftermath. The photographs reward sustained attention while resisting the quick authority of spectacle.

The photographers carry that inquiry across three generations of Magnum practice. Seymour represents the cooperative’s founding commitment to the authored eyewitness. Towell and Bar-Am developed sustained practices shaped by long involvement with the places they photographed. Saman and Meloni foreground the conditions of contemporary conflict photography: controlled access, official narrative, uncertain evidence, archival recurrence, and the afterlife of violence. The five political settings remain distinct; their relationship lies in the changing ways photographers have worked around conflict and interpreted its traces.

Four works came through Azu Nwagbogu’s second Magnum 75 curation, which examined photographs charged with unresolved meaning. Saman’s work came through Alejandro Cartagena’s first selection and locates uncertainty in the caption as well as the image. The official claim, visible damage, conditions of access, and limits of proof remain separately available for scrutiny. Our grouping develops an independent proposition from those source selections: conflict is pictured through its edges and interpreted through the records that surround it.

The token editions add another history. Photographs made for magazines, agencies, books, prints, and exhibitions were issued in 2022 as individually identified 1/1 tokens with public metadata, persistent media links, and Ethereum provenance. Each token connects an archive number and historical caption to a media file, contract, and transfer history. The resulting object allows the Museum to distinguish what the photograph shows, what its caption asserts, what the token identifies, and what later scholarship establishes.

For the 6529 Network Museum, the gift joins postwar documentary history to chain-native cultural history at the level of exact objects. It broadens the collection beyond generative systems while remaining specific to the Museum’s medium. Across sixty-four years, five practices, and two source selections, the group supports close study without proposing a single formula for conflict photography. The case for selection therefore rests on two inseparable subjects: five individual photographs and the institutional moment in which Magnum carried its archive into blockchain-based publication and collecting.

## The countercase

The group is donor-formed, bringing five distinct practices, countries, decades, assignments, and political conditions under one interpretation. The claim that these photographs meet at the edges of conflict comes after the works, and voters may judge that it connects unlike events too smoothly.

Four photographs concern Israel, Jerusalem, Libya, or Syria; the fifth concerns El Salvador. This concentration can deepen a study of conflict photography, but it can also repeat a narrow visual economy in which regions are represented principally through soldiers, damage, sacred conflict, and ruin. The group offers no corresponding account of everyday life, resistance outside armed frames, or the people represented beyond the event caption.

Saman's photograph places a child within an evidentiary frame. The record cannot establish the child's identity, consent, or agency. Voters may consider whether its display repeats the extractive logic the proposal seeks to examine.

Several exact histories remain incomplete. Seymour's commissioner and first publication are unknown. Towell's church and assignment remain unidentified. Bar-Am's exact date is unresolved. Saman's official account was unverifiable. Meloni worked under government permission. These are meaningful conditions of the photographs, though voters may consider the record too incomplete for the proposed grouping.

All five metadata records state **All Rights Reserved**. The available terms convey ERC-721 control without a copyright, reproduction, publication, display, derivative, or preservation grant. The public Arweave files are available and fixed by hash. Museum preservation copies and a completed rights review remain outstanding. Voters may weigh the group's documentary interest against the limits on future reproduction and preservation.

## A response to the countercase

The donor formed the group, and the proposal identifies that authorship openly. Museums regularly bring independent works into relation; the validity of such a group rests on the precision of those relations. Here they are concrete: one Magnum archive, one anniversary project, one token contract, two named curatorial selections, five documentary practices, and sustained attention to the structures surrounding conflict. Magnum and the photographers retain their own authorship; the group and its title belong to this proposal.

The geographic concentration sets a clear limit. This is a focused study of how one photographic institution and five of its photographers have pictured authority, evidence, and aftermath. It cannot stand for Israel, Palestine, El Salvador, Libya, Syria, or conflict photography as a whole. Museum interpretation should preserve that scale, identify lives and histories absent from the group, and avoid presenting five works as a general portrait of the places depicted.

Unidentified people, incomplete consent records, disputed captions, managed access, and uncertain causation recur throughout documentary photography, particularly in war and civil unrest. They carry ethical consequences that belong in acquisition, cataloguing, and display. Saman’s image requires special care because it depicts a child whose identity and consent are unrecorded. The catalogue should preserve that uncertainty. Display should avoid an invented biography, any claim of consent, and sensational treatment, while giving viewers enough context to understand how officials presented the scene to the photographer.

The unresolved assignment and publication histories define the research agenda. All five works have exact contract and token identities, archive references, captions, and fixed media observations; several also have matching artist or museum records. Documentary archives often remain incomplete at acquisition. The Museum can preserve the open questions in the catalogue and continue research without converting uncertainty into fact.

Retained copyright is customary in museum collecting, and ownership of a work ordinarily remains separate from copyright. Digital photography makes display, reproduction, and preservation permissions especially consequential. The All Rights Reserved notices define a required rights review after selection and before acceptance.

These conditions establish the proper scale of the acquisition: a five-work study with explicit limits, careful treatment of the child and political settings, an open research record, and completed rights and preservation work before accession. They also set an exacting standard for the Museum’s first sustained engagement with documentary photography.
