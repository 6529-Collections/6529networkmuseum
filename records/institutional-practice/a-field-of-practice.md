# A field of practice

- **Subtitle:** Institutions the 6529 Network Museum studies
- **Status:** public scholarship
- **Institutional author:** 6529 Network Museum
- **Version:** 1.0.1
- **Publication date:** 2026-08-04
- **Research cutoff:** 2026-08-04

This study begins with problems the 6529 Network Museum must solve: how to
publish a collection as a research resource; how to
show an artwork whose medium is software or a network; how to document change
without erasing history; how to give provenance, rights, and conservation
evidence their proper weight; how to invite correction; and how to make serious
scholarship readable beyond a specialist circle.

The institutions gathered here are not a ranking. They were selected because
their public work—collection records, archives, conservation studies, datasets,
publications, commissions, or tools—offers evidence on one or more of those
problems.

The field is deliberately mixed. The Met, MoMA, the Whitney, Tate, Centre
Pompidou, SFMOMA, the Guggenheim, the V&A, and LACMA are collecting museums
with very different histories and remits. Getty contributes a connected
research, conservation, vocabulary, data, and publishing infrastructure. ZKM
and Ars Electronica grew around media art, production, research, and public
experimentation. Rhizome began as an online community and made preservation
software part of its institutional practice. Serpentine Arts Technologies
operates through commissions, laboratories, research, and tools. Their scale,
funding, audiences, and physical infrastructure differ from one another and
from ours. The differences keep admiration from becoming imitation.

## What we looked for

Reputation was not evidence. Each profile begins from public work: collection
records, exhibition and program archives, conservation case studies, technical
documentation, research publications, source code or datasets, and the
structure of the institution's own catalogue. Mission statements establish
ambition; they do not establish achievement.

Six practices recur across the material reviewed.

### The object record is a doorway

An authoritative record identifies a work and opens routes to interpretation,
provenance, exhibition history, bibliography, conservation, related works, and
reusable data. [Centre Pompidou's object pages](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/ressources/oeuvre/coXyg5p)
and [the Met's layered records](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/900397)
show how a compact identity can lead into a research trail. The Whitney's
presentation of Douglas Davis's [*The World's First Collaborative Sentence*](https://whitney.org/artport/douglas-davis)
brings the artwork, conservation history, unresolved defects, artist statement,
provenance, and public contribution route into one account.

### Digital art is a changing technical arrangement

Pam and Dick Kramlich sponsored [*Matters in Media Art*](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/matters-in-media-art/)
in 2003; the New Art Trust, MoMA, SFMOMA, and Tate developed the international
research collaboration around the acquisition, loan, installation, and care of
time-based work. The Guggenheim's [computer-based art initiative](https://www.guggenheim.org/conservation/the-conserving-computer-based-art-initiative)
documents software, hardware, dependencies, behavior, source code, and installed
iterations. ZKM's [restoration program](https://zkm.de/en/restoration-of-electronic-and-digital-art)
joins conservators, computer scientists, engineers, and art historians.
Technical analysis in these programs contributes evidence about how the artwork
behaves and which properties its stewards must preserve.

### Preservation includes an account of intervention

A migrated or emulated work requires an account of intervention. Tate's
[reconstruction of its Net Art Commission records](https://archive.tate.org.uk/Record.aspx?id=TG+100%2F1&src=CalmView.Catalog)
discloses loss, recovered sources, interviews, archival order, and remaining
gaps. Rhizome's *Net Art Anthology* published preservation matrices and used
web-archiving and emulation tools. Rhizome's [May 2026 service
notice](https://blog.conifer.rhizome.org/2026/05/06/twilight-webinar.html)
records the end of paid Conifer subscriptions; its published transition plan
scheduled replacement of the platform with a read-only tombstone application
in June. The current [Conifer service
notice](https://conifer.rhizome.org/) reports that creating and modifying
collections was disabled before discontinuation and directs users to the
transition record. The Guggenheim's identity and iteration reports distinguish
the work's enduring properties from a particular technical realization. These
accounts let the public inspect changes, decisions, and supporting evidence.

### An archive can be an active public instrument

MoMA's [exhibition history](https://www.moma.org/calendar/about/exhibition-history)
can connect exhibition records to catalogues, installation views, artists, and
primary documents across nine decades while identifying incomplete film and
performance coverage. Ars Electronica reports that its [archive](https://ars.electronica.art/archive/en/about/)
joins festival programs, Prix submissions, audiovisual documentation,
publications, and records of changing storage media. Rhizome's [*Net Art
Anthology*](https://old.rhizome.org/editorial/2019/jan/28/the-making-of-net-art-anthology-rhizome-and-google-arts-culture/)
used conservation research to make historical works encounterable again. Each
archive gives later scholarship material it can test and reinterpret.

### Publishing can be infrastructure

Several programs publish finished essays alongside datasets, catalogues,
vocabularies, methods, forms, checklists, code, and research tools. MoMA and the
Met make collection data available for reuse while
stating its incompleteness. The Guggenheim publishes acquisition and disk-
imaging resources. The Getty's work on online scholarly catalogues shows that
digital publication can carry notes, media, updates, and multiple reading
paths while remaining citable. Serpentine's laboratories turn research into
reports and practical resources for artists and institutions.

### Public limitations are part of the record

Several cited pages state what the institution does not know or
has not yet solved. Tate records accidental archival loss. The Whitney names a
character-encoding defect that remains unresolved. The V&A describes a digital
conservation practice while it is still being built. MoMA labels collection
data that has not received curatorial approval. These admissions do not weaken
the record. They let a reader judge its condition.

## What this changes here

The study sharpens responsibilities already present in Museum standards and
identifies practices those standards should absorb.

1. **Every accessioned work should have a public research path.** Identity,
   close looking, provenance, rights, technical
   constitution, conservation history, bibliography, and related works should
   remain distinguishable and connected.
2. **Executable works require identity and iteration records.** Source,
   dependencies, runtime, display behavior, artist decisions, captured states,
   and every consequential intervention belong to the work's history.
3. **Corrections must remain visible.** A stronger record cites and supersedes
   the earlier assertion; it does not make the earlier state disappear.
4. **Scholarship must show its support.** Notes should bind factual claims to
   exact sources. Interpretation should make its argument from the work; an
   institutional byline cannot make the argument on its own.
5. **The public catalogue should be reusable.** Stable identifiers, open
   schemas, content commitments, exports, and clear rights make independent
   research possible.
6. **The interface and the record have different lifespans.** Pages should
   make art inviting to encounter. Durable records should let another
   interface reproduce the Museum's claims and history without trusting this
   one.

These are working lessons. A practice enters the Museum only when it answers
our collection, our public, and the particular demands of chain-native and
executable art. The profiles explain what each institution
has demonstrated, what we can use, and where the analogy ends.
