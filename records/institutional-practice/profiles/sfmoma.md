SFMOMA’s [Media Arts](https://www.sfmoma.org/artists-artworks/media-arts/)
page states that its Media Arts department was established in 1987. The page
lists video, film, slide, sound, computer-based, online, and live-performance
works, and connects collection highlights to online artworks, publications, and
media-preservation research. Its collection record for [Harrell Fletcher and
Miranda July; Yuri Ono and web designer, *Learning to Love You More*,
2002–2009](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/2010.45.A-EEEEEEEE/) identifies the
work as digital media, a web project and archive, acquired in 2010, and not on
view at the time of the record.

## Questions asked at acquisition

Jill Sterrett’s [“Matters in Media
Art”](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/matters-in-media-art/) describes media
artworks as dormant in storage and activated by viewers in the gallery. The
essay records SFMOMA’s need to ask artists about elaborate installation
setups, specialized technology, viewing conditions, and care appropriate to
the work’s intentions.

The same essay identifies the 1996 Team Media group as initially “a registrar,
a media tech, and me,” in Sterrett’s account. SFMOMA’s [“Team Media: In Action,
in Contemplation”](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/team-media-action-contemplation/)
describes its monthly meeting as bringing together curators, conservators,
technicians, intellectual-property managers, registrars, and web and digital
staff. It says the group began with three members and regularly attracts more
than fifteen interested people.

SFMOMA presents [“Matters in Media Art”](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/matters-in-media-art/) as an international collaboration among
the New Art Trust, The Museum of Modern Art, SFMOMA, and Tate. The essay says
the collaboration produced procedures and downloadable forms for lending
video, film, audio, and computer-based installations, and that practices are
adopted and revised as technology changes.

For the 6529 Network Museum, the accession gate for an executable work should
require a completed technical questionnaire with: artist installation
instructions; hardware, software, and service dependencies; significant
properties; active and dormant test results; named change authority; artist or
estate contacts; rights to preserve source and dependencies; and an approved
fallback manifestation. A cross-functional review should sign the record
before accession, and each later revision should cite the case that prompted
it.

## Writing from practice

SFMOMA publishes two complementary editorial forms. The [*Learning to Love You
More* collection record](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/2010.45.A-EEEEEEEE/)
combines a concise object description with classification, medium, dates,
acquisition, credit, copyright, permanent URL, and display status. Sterrett’s
longer [“Matters in Media Art”](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/matters-in-media-art/)
names the staff group, the acquisition questions, the partner institutions,
and the downloadable forms. Together, the pages connect a stable object
record to the institutional reasoning around its care.

The Museum should use the same editorial division: keep facts and status in
the object record, and publish the technical decisions, participants,
uncertainties, and revision history in linked practitioner essays.

## What the Museum should adopt

- Add the technical questionnaire and cross-functional approval to the
  accession checklist for every executable or service-dependent work.
- Store separate dormant and activated condition observations, each with date,
  environment, test result, and evidence link.
- Assign a named authority for hardware substitution, software migration,
  dependency replacement, and fallback display.
- Publish reusable forms with version numbers, adoption dates, and amendment
  notes tied to specific casework.
- Create a standing media review that includes curation, conservation,
  registration, technical operations, rights, and web or digital staff.

## Where the analogy ends

SFMOMA’s Team Media account presupposes a staffed museum with registrars,
conservators, technicians, rights staff, and digital staff who can meet
regularly. A decentralized museum must name equivalent roles, fund outside
partners where needed, and record responsibility in each object file. The
downloadable form does not settle artist-specific rights or significant
properties; those remain work-level decisions.
