Tate's Net Art Commissions record makes archival loss and reconstruction part of
the public record. The [Tate Public Records catalogue](https://archive.tate.org.uk/Record.aspx?id=TG+100%2F1&src=CalmView.Catalog)
says that most of the original records were accidentally lost between 2016 and
2018. It separates recovered original material, reconstituted records, and an
archived version of the Intermedia Art microsite. It also names the recovery
sources and states that a cataloguer created an artificial order because the
original order could not be followed. The catalogue record itself demonstrates
the archival practice.

## Close reading: Net Art Commissions and the Intermedia Art Microsite

The [series record](https://archive.tate.org.uk/Record.aspx?id=TG+100%2F1&src=CalmView.Catalog)
identifies fifteen internet and software-based artworks commissioned between
2000 and 2012. The [Intermedia Art Microsite record](https://archive.tate.org.uk/Record.aspx?id=TG+100%2F1%2F9&src=CalmView.Catalog)
describes the 2008–2012 microsite that gathered those works with contextual
texts, interviews, events, broadcasts, and podcasts.

Its conservation description is unusually exact. Tate records a Conifer capture
made between November 2019 and February 2020, explains that the capture was
organized by artwork and other site sections, says that only the first page of
external links was captured, and records the merging of section-level WARC
files. It also names the failures: three works were unavailable because their
artists no longer hosted them; *Uncomfortable Proximity* could not be
experienced in full; and the podcasts were not archived. The record therefore
describes a bounded access manifestation and states its omissions.

For the 6529 Network Museum, a reconstructed record should expose the same
distinctions:

- record whether each assertion is original, recovered, reconstituted, or
  derived from a later capture;
- publish the source inventory, reconstruction method, artificial order,
  author, date, confidence, and superseded record;
- state capture date, scope, external-link policy, package/fixity reference,
  runtime or emulation conditions, unavailable components, and current access
  status;
- keep token identity, metadata, dependencies, platform behavior, community
  context, and exhibition history as separate assertions.

## Research and conservation: Reshaping the Collectible

The archive identifies [Reshaping the Collectible: The Lives of Net Art](https://www.tate.org.uk/research/reshaping-the-collectible#the-lives-of-net-art)
as the 2021 Tate Research Publication that supplied the research context for
reconstituting the lost records. It also names Lucy Bayley's
[“Net Art: Dusty Navigational Pathways”](https://www.tate.org.uk/research/reshaping-the-collectible/net-art-dusty-navigational-pathways)
as a chapter in that publication. The archive further records that Tate's
Public Records and Time-Based Media Conservation teams worked together on the
microsite and reconstitution process.

The writing's strength is procedural: it identifies what was found, what was
reconstructed, how the order was made, and what remains unavailable. That
method gives a chain-native museum a usable scholarly form for uncertainty.
Disclosure of recovery and intervention is the practice to adopt. The record
treats web capture as a partial manifestation with documented boundaries.

## What the Museum should adopt

- publish reconstruction lineage as part of the record, not as an invisible
  editorial process;
- bind every public technical claim to a dated capture, source package, or
  observation;
- record the authority and rationale for consequential changes to metadata,
  dependencies, runtime, or display behavior;
- expose incomplete access and unresolved components beside the public
  manifestation;
- issue a new, attributable record when a later reconstruction supersedes an
  earlier one.

## Where the analogy ends

Tate's evidence concerns a historical public-records workflow and a specific
2018–2022 research project. It does not show that every networked artwork can
be recovered, that every capture is complete, or that the historical capture
tool remains available. The Museum should adopt the explicit boundaries and
lineage, then test its own preservation packages against its own works and
dependencies.
