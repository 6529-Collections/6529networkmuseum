- **Research cutoff:** 2026-08-04

Robert Smithson's *Spiral Jetty* is published by Dia as both a collection object
and a changing site. The [collection record](https://www.diaart.org/collection/collection/smithson-robert-spiral-jetty-1970-1999-014)
identifies the work as 1970, gives its materials as black basalt rock, salt
crystals, earth, and water, records its approximate dimensions as 18,000 by 180
inches, assigns reference number 1999.014, and locates it at the Great Salt
Lake. The record keeps material identity, site, credit, and interpretive
context together without treating the site as a static image.

## A site-specific record with a longitudinal condition view

Dia's [project page for *Spiral Jetty*](https://www.diaart.org/exhibition/exhibitions-projects/robert-smithson-spiral-jetty-site/)
describes Dia's stewardship, visitor conditions, environmental change, public
partners, rights, and the work's relation to water level and entropy. It also
states that Dia has commissioned aerial documentation once or twice a year in
spring and fall since 2012. The [aerial documentation index](https://www.diaart.org/collection/spiraljettyaerials)
lists dated images through Spring 2026.

The decisive practice is serial observation. Dia does not make one undated
condition statement stand for the work's entire history; it publishes a chain
of observations tied to place and date. Its prose moves from object identity to
site, stewardship, visitor access, environmental circumstance, and repeated
documentation. That is a stronger public record for a changing work than a
single descriptive paragraph, but it remains a physical-site method.

For the 6529 Network Museum, a site-, environment-, or service-dependent work
should have separate linked records for identity, current condition,
observation method, location or runtime, access constraints, image or data
rights, responsible parties, and unresolved change. A recurring observation
should record the date, instrument or method, source file or image, operator,
conditions, and relation to the previous observation. The Museum should never
turn a later observation into an undated claim about the work's original state.

## History and publication as institutional evidence

Dia's [history page](https://www.diaart.org/about/historyofdia) uses an
interactive timeline for its fiftieth anniversary, combining archive documents,
photographs, video, ephemera, and the voices of artists, curators, and
caretakers. Dia's [publication page](https://www.diaart.org/shop/dia-publications)
describes artist monographs, exhibition and collection catalogues, artist books,
poetry, readers, and critical theory, with named editors and contributors.

The publication genre is work-centered and sustained. A collection object is
not asked to carry the entire institutional history; the object record, site
project, serial documentation, archive timeline, and longer publication each
perform a different editorial task. Dia's contact page states that its archive
is a comprehensive repository of institutional and program history undergoing
multi-year processing, preservation, and access work, and is not currently
available for research. The Museum should therefore distinguish an archive's
existence from public research access.

## What the Museum should adopt

- Use a dated observation series for changing physical or networked works.
- Separate object identity, site or runtime, stewardship, visitor access,
  condition, rights, and serial documentation.
- Publish archive timelines from named primary materials and identify the
  voices or roles represented in each historical account.
- Treat artist/work publications, collection records, and institutional history
  as linked but distinct genres.
- Mark archive processing and access status explicitly.

## Where the analogy ends

Dia's evidence concerns a land-based sculpture, environmental change, recurring
aerial photography, and a publication and archive program. It does not
demonstrate digital-art conservation, token preservation, software migration,
or chain-data governance. The Museum should adopt the dated-observation and
work-centered publication structures without importing Dia's physical-site
custodial assumptions.
