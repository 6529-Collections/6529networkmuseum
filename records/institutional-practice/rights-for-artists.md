# Rights for artists

A rights statement is part of an artwork's public life. It tells collectors,
museums, publishers, teachers, and other artists what they may do with the
work. A clear statement can widen access, protect an artistic boundary, or do
both at once.

This guide begins with the decisions an artist can make before minting or
publishing a digital work. The final terms should reflect the work, its
components, and the artist's intentions.

## Public access has a long art history

Artists already work in a world formed by the public domain. Centuries of
painting, sculpture, printmaking, literature, and music remain available
because copyright is limited in duration. Hokusai could study imported prints;
later artists could return to Hokusai. Contemporary artists can quote,
translate, reproduce, and transform public-domain works without negotiating a
copyright license for the underlying work.

CC0 extends that possibility to a new work by choice. It can suit an artist who
wants broad circulation, unconstrained remaking, or a work designed to become
shared cultural material immediately. The choice is substantial, yet its
practical setting is familiar: many of the most studied and reproduced works
in museums are already in the public domain.

A public-domain source still deserves accurate identification. A modern
photograph, translation, recording, or edition may carry its own copyright,
and trademark, privacy, publicity, cultural-heritage, or contractual limits may
remain.

## Start with the work

List the elements that a future custodian would need to understand:

- the visible or audible output;
- the live program and its source code;
- text, sound, video, typefaces, data, and interfaces;
- libraries, models, APIs, and other dependencies;
- instructions, scores, certificates, and installation material;
- contributions by collaborators, fabricators, performers, or commissioners.

Each element needs a source and rights status. A Creative Commons license on
an image does not automatically cover a third-party font or library. A code
license may govern source code while a Creative Commons license governs the
artistic output and documentation.

## Confirm that you can grant the rights

Copyright usually begins with the author, subject to national rules for
employment, works made for hire, commissions, and collaboration. Review any
agreement that shaped the work. Clear samples, stock material, datasets,
typefaces, music, software, and commissioned contributions before promising
rights to others.

For a collaborative work, record who owns each component and who may license
it. A single person should not apply a public license on behalf of every
contributor without authority.

## Decide what kind of circulation you want

Ask concrete questions:

- May people post faithful copies?
- May a museum publish the work in a catalogue?
- May teachers include it in course material?
- May conservators migrate or emulate it?
- May another artist publish a remix or translation?
- Must a shared adaptation remain under the same terms?
- May the work appear in paid publications, commercial exhibitions,
  merchandise, advertising, or training material?

The answers point toward an instrument. CC0 offers the broadest public reuse.
CC BY requires credit. CC BY-SA also keeps shared adaptations under open
terms. NC restricts the licensed grant to noncommercial uses. ND withholds
permission to share adaptations. A custom license can express a genuinely
different intention, but it places a greater reading and preservation burden
on everyone who encounters the work.

## Understand the NC and ND choices

**NonCommercial** concerns the purpose of a use. A museum, foundation, or
university may conduct commercial activities; a company may conduct a
noncommercial one. Ticketed exhibitions, sponsored presentations, paid
catalogues, image-licensing services, and merchandise deserve an explicit
answer if they matter to you.

**NoDerivatives** allows people to make adaptations privately but withholds
permission to share the adapted material. For time-based and software-based
art, decide how that boundary should work with migration, emulation,
accessibility, translation, restoration, and replacement of obsolete
dependencies. Creative Commons 4.0 permits technical modifications necessary
to exercise the licensed rights, yet a substantial change can still create
adapted material.

If conservation flexibility matters and public remixes do not, a separate
preservation permission can accompany an ND license.

## Treat the token and copyright separately

State what a token holder receives. Useful terms may address personal display,
public display, lending, resale listings, social media, commercial use,
preservation access, and transfer of holder-specific rights with the token.

A museum or collector will ordinarily understand the acquisition of an
artwork to include faithful display in the form for which it was made. If the
work needs a particular screen, browser, live endpoint, wallet state, source
file, or installation sequence, describe it as part of the work. If you intend
to limit ordinary display, collection documentation, or technical
preservation, say so expressly before the sale. Silence creates uncertainty
about the boundary of stewardship; it should never operate as a hidden ban on
showing the acquired work.

The public license and holder rights can coexist. A CC license grants rights to
everyone. A holder license grants additional rights to the current token owner.
The documents should say how holder rights begin, end, and follow a transfer.

A token sale alone does not transfer copyright. In the United States, for
example, ownership of copyright is legally distinct from ownership of the
material object in which a work is embodied. A copyright transfer needs the
form required by the applicable law.

## Apply the license where people will find it

Record at least:

- the full license name and version;
- its canonical URI;
- the artist or other licensor;
- the components covered;
- a preferred credit line;
- the date and place of first publication under those terms;
- the exact metadata, contract, platform terms, or signed document that proves
  the license applies;
- any separate permissions for token holders, museums, or conservators.

Put the information in durable metadata and in a human-readable project page.
For an on-chain work, consider recording a content hash or immutable URI for
the operative terms. Keep a copy outside the platform as well.

Creative Commons 4.0 licenses are irrevocable for a recipient who complies.
An artist may offer the same material under additional terms later, or stop
distributing new copies, without withdrawing the existing public grant from
compliant users.

## Write a useful credit line

A practical credit line identifies the artist, title, date, collection or
project when relevant, license, and source. The license also requires retention
of supplied copyright and license notices and an indication of changes.

Example structure:

> Artist, *Title*, year. Licensed CC BY 4.0. Source: canonical project URI.
> Cropped from the original.

The exact form can be adapted to the medium. Keep it easy to copy.

## Plan for preservation

Digital works change when browsers, operating systems, APIs, hardware, and
networks change. Decide which actions a future custodian may take:

- copy files into managed storage;
- inspect and document source code;
- replace a dependency or endpoint;
- migrate a file format;
- emulate hardware or software;
- make an accessibility version;
- record the work in operation;
- exhibit a restored or artist-approved version.

A public license may already permit much of this. A short artist-approved
preservation statement can resolve the remaining ambiguity and distinguish an
authentic conservation intervention from a new adaptation.

## Keep an artist's rights packet

The most useful packet contains:

1. a work and component inventory;
2. authorship and collaboration records;
3. the exact public license or custom terms;
4. token-holder terms, if any;
5. preferred credits and notices;
6. third-party licenses and dependency versions;
7. display and preservation instructions;
8. hashes or other fixity for the work and operative documents;
9. a contact and succession plan for future technical or rights questions.

This packet makes a sale, loan, gift, exhibition, and future conservation
project easier. It also lets an institution describe the artist's choices
accurately.
