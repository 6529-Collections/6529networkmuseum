# WP-3 research memo: Larry Towell, Magnum Photos 75 #145

**Scope.** Research for the proposed-object corpus entry concerning the exact Magnum Photos 75 token identified locally as token `145`, with the photographic work captioned “Government soldiers in a church, Suchitoto, El Salvador,” 1986. This memo is research only. It does not establish accession, acceptance, donation completion, copyright transfer, or Museum collection status.

**Research access date.** 2026-08-08 UTC. URLs and access dates are listed in the source register below. Web pages that were inaccessible are recorded as unresolved rather than filled from inference.

## Bottom line

The local proposal and the token metadata agree on the underlying token reference, artist, year, archive number, 1/1 edition, curation, linked image, and rights notice. The token’s source metadata uses the place spelling **Suchitito**; local proposal and institutional records normalize this to **Suchitoto**. The archive number is `TOL1986003W00045/26`.

An Art Windsor-Essex collection record provides a strong institutional parallel: Larry Towell, *El Salvador Series 1986-1987 (Soldiers in Church, Suchitoto, El Salvador)*, 1986, gelatin silver print, 35.0 x 28.0 cm, collection number `2002.112`. The public institutional page does not publish the Magnum archive number, so equivalence between that physical print and token #145 is not independently proven here.

The image visibly shows two armed soldiers standing inside a church beneath a crucifix. The Magnum/token caption places the church near the Guazapa guerrilla stronghold and attributes persecution of the Catholic Church to its liberation-theology influence. Authoritative historical sources corroborate a broad context of church persecution, church social action, and fighting around Guazapa in the relevant period; they do **not** identify this church, these soldiers, the unit, the operation, or a scene-specific cause. Those details remain unresolved.

Towell’s Central America practice and the early exhibition/book history are well supported. The specific assignment or commission for this frame, the first publication of this frame, its page in the 1997 book *El Salvador*, and any adjacent contact-sheet sequence are not established by the sources located.

## Visible facts and technical observation

**Visible facts / technical observation (Museum evidence class C).** Inspection of the token-linked JPEG and the local caption supports the following literal description:

Two soldiers face the camera in the lower half of a largely symmetrical church interior. Each holds a rifle angled away from the body. A crucifix rises between and above them. Light enters through windows on both sides, and a small arrangement of flowers appears low near the altar. The composition places armed bodies, sacred architecture, and a central vertical crucifix into a balanced, nearly frontal arrangement.

This is a description of the linked JPEG rendition, not a condition report on a physical print and not a claim about the original negative. The visual evidence does not identify the church, the soldiers, their unit, the reason for their presence, whether the image was made during an operation, or whether the church was occupied, searched, guarded, or otherwise used for a particular purpose.

**Not visible / not established.** The frame does not by itself establish a church name, town subdivision, military formation, date more precise than 1986, operation name, photographer’s exact position, or the causal relationship asserted in the caption. Do not convert the crucifix, soldiers, or rifles into a more specific historical claim without a source that identifies it.

## Towell practice and assignment context

**Institutional/agency biography.** The official Magnum store profile says Towell studied visual arts at York University in Toronto from 1972 to 1976, volunteered in Calcutta, taught folk music in the 1980s, documented the Nicaraguan Contra war and relatives of people disappeared in Guatemala, joined Magnum in 1988, and worked as a freelance photographer. It lists holdings of his work at the Getty, National Gallery of Canada, George Eastman House, National Museum of Qatar, and Archive of Modern Conflict. This is useful practice context, not object-specific provenance.

The Art Canada Institute describes Towell as an independent photojournalist from 1984 whose work addressed conflict, resistance, dispossession, and survival. Its account places his 1980s Central America work in relation to civilians and the FMLN in El Salvador, army activity and displacement, and human-rights violations in Guatemala. This institutional scholarly account supports the broad subject and practice context but does not identify the target frame’s assignment or publication.

**Artist statement.** In a 2019 Momus interview, Towell describes his work as ethically motivated witnessing and says that he generally worked on his own projects; Magnum sometimes distributed work to magazines and sometimes supplied assignments, but much of his work was not generated by Magnum assignments. He also discusses landlessness as a recurring subject and the importance of composition, balance, focal points, and lines. These are Towell’s statements about practice. They should not be used to infer that #145 was independently made, commissioned by Magnum, or made for a particular publication.

**Bounded assignment conclusion.** The sources establish that Towell was making sustained Central America work and that Magnum was an agency/distribution context. They do not establish whether this exact frame came from a magazine commission, a personal project, a human-rights delegation, or another assignment. Keep the assignment field unresolved until an archive, contact sheet, notebook, agency record, book colophon, or direct artist/estate statement links this frame to one.

Membership-date wording varies among institutional materials: the official Magnum store says he joined in 1988; the Henri Cartier-Bresson Foundation dossier says “since 1989”; other institutional/bookstore copy describes nominee/full-member milestones differently. This discrepancy is not material to the object identity and should not be silently resolved.

## Salvadoran civil-war and church context

**Caption/agency statement.** The Magnum/token caption situates the image near the Guazapa guerrilla stronghold and connects Catholic Church persecution to liberation theology. This wording should be retained as an attributed caption statement, not rewritten as an unqualified scene fact.

**Authoritative historical corroboration.** The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights documented killings and attacks against priests and other Catholic Church members, including attacks on church institutions and an army entry into a Catholic church while seeking a priest. A later IACHR merits report describes systematic harassment of clergy and lay people involved in church social action. These reports corroborate the broader historical proposition that Catholic Church social action and religious actors were subject to persecution in El Salvador; they do not identify the church or people in Towell’s frame.

The Library of Congress country study describes the influence of Vatican II and the Medellín conference on the Salvadoran Church’s social orientation and its “preferential option for the poor,” providing authoritative background for the liberation-theology reference. Again, this is contextual history, not evidence that the specific church in the photograph was targeted for that reason.

United Nations human-rights reporting documents the 1986 fighting and displacement around the Guazapa area, including an army operation and evacuation of civilians. This supports describing Guazapa as a contested wartime setting in 1986. It does not prove that the soldiers in this interior were part of that operation or that the frame was made during it.

**Museum interpretation (bounded).** The image can be interpreted as placing military authority in visual tension with a Catholic sacred interior and the local caption’s account of church persecution. That interpretation should remain visibly separate from the historical facts and from the caption’s scene-specific causal language.

## Publication, exhibition, and book history

The sources establish the following history for Towell’s Central America and El Salvador work:

- The archival exhibition record for *Larry Towell: Gifts of War: An Exhibition of Photographs from Nicaragua, El Salvador, Guatemala* identifies Grant Arnold as author/curator, the Art Gallery of Windsor as organizer, and an itinerary from 16 April 1988 through 14 October 1989 across venues in Windsor, London, Saskatoon, Sarnia, and Toronto. The record describes photographs and testimonies concerning peasant/civilian repression and terrorism. It does not list individual images, so it does not prove that #145 was shown.
- Towell’s CV lists *Gift of War* (Coach House Press, Toronto, 1988) and *El Salvador* (W.W. Norton/DoubleTake Books, 1997).
- Library catalogs identify *El Salvador* as a 1997 first-edition, 127-page photobook by Towell, with an introduction by Mark Danner in the cataloged edition; subjects include violence, insurgency, and the history of El Salvador, 1979–1992. Catalog copy says Towell first traveled to El Salvador in 1986 as part of a human-rights delegation and documented the war and aftermath for approximately a decade. Treat that as catalog/institutional copy, not as direct proof of the target frame’s assignment.
- Stephen Bulger Gallery records a 1997 *El Salvador* exhibition. The public overview uses another image as its example and does not establish that #145 was exhibited there.
- No source located in this pass provides a page number, plate number, or other direct crosswalk showing that the exact #145 frame appears in *El Salvador* (1997), *Gift of War* (1988), or a named exhibition.
