# WP-3 research memo: Lorenzo Meloni, Magnum Photos 75 #104

Research cut-off and source access date: 2026-08-08. Local proposal and fixity observations cited below were recorded in the repository on 2026-08-05 unless another date is stated. This is a research memo only. The local record is a proposed-gift corpus; nothing here establishes Museum accession, acceptance, custody, copyright transfer, or a reproduction licence.

## Executive finding

The exact object is the issuer-described Magnum 75 token sequence 104/225, titled `Palmyra, Syria`, by Lorenzo Meloni, made on 1 April 2016, with Magnum archive number `MEL2016002G0401/4032`. The date and caption are independently anchored by TIME's 7 April 2016 photo essay, whose caption identifies a Syrian army soldier removing or lifting his helmet while seated on the rubble of the former Temple of Bel in ancient Palmyra. The frame is an aftermath image made five days after Syrian government and allied forces retook Palmyra from Islamic State control, not a record of the Temple of Bel's destruction itself.

The access context matters. TIME reports that Meloni obtained Syrian government permission to enter Palmyra and spent the day with government forces; contemporary AFP reporting describes the same post-recapture site as mined, with entry permitted only along army-directed paths while demining continued. That evidence supports describing the photograph as made under controlled military access, not as an unmediated view of the site. It does not establish the photographer's exact route, the identity of the soldier, a specific permit, or any endorsement of the Syrian government account.

The picture's caption binds a small, obscured human gesture to a damaged World Heritage site. Meloni's own and institutional descriptions place such images within a longer, historicist and fragmentary practice about the rise, rule, fall, and aftermath of Islamic State and the longer histories of intervention and division in the region. That is a curatorial reading of the object's context, not evidence that the image itself shows the 2015 attack, the recapture battle, or the soldier's personal experience.

## 2. Visible facts and disciplined non-claims

### Visible / captioned facts

- The frame is a color photograph in a wide horizontal composition. Pale stone and rubble occupy the foreground and middle ground; hard daylight and a clear blue sky are visible.
- A single seated uniformed figure occupies the lower portion of the frame. The figure raises or removes a helmet across the face; the face is not available for identification in the image. A long firearm appears with the figure. These are visual observations; “Syrian army soldier” is the contemporary publication's caption, not an identity established by the pixels alone.
- Standing columns and a surviving portico/colonnade remain behind the figure. The frame does not itself label the monument. TIME's caption identifies the rubble as the former Temple of Bel; UNESCO's site and damage records supply the archaeological and destruction context.
- The image shows a pause or gesture in a damaged monumental landscape. It does not show an explosion, the moment of demolition, combat, the recapture operation, an Islamic State fighter, a civilian witness, or a named soldier.

### What the image does not establish

- It does not show the destruction of the Temple of Bel on 30 August 2015. That date and event come from UNESCO's eyewitness and satellite-image report, not from the frame.
- It does not independently prove who destroyed the temple. UNESCO's rapid mission uses “armed groups” in its control and damage chronology; the token metadata, TIME, Guardian, and contemporary reporting identify the relevant destroyers as Islamic State / IS / ISIS. Preserve that source distinction rather than treating an image caption as a forensic attribution.
- It does not show the 27 March 2016 recapture battle or prove that the pictured soldier participated in it. The five-day temporal relationship is documentary context supplied by TIME and UNESCO.
- It does not identify the figure's name, rank, unit, affiliation beyond the published caption, emotional state, or reason for the helmet gesture.
- It does not establish an exact coordinate, camera position, route into the ruins, military permit document, or whether the pictured site was already fully demined. It also does not establish that every visible column survived the conflict undamaged outside the frame.
- It does not grant copyright, reproduction, display, or commercial rights. Token ownership and the `1 of 1` edition statement are not copyright transfer or Museum acceptance.

## 3. Palmyra chronology and military access context

### Chronology

1. UNESCO reported on 1 September 2015 that eyewitness reports and UNOSAT satellite imagery confirmed explosives destroyed the Temple of Bel on 30 August 2015. This supplies the exact pre-image destruction date.
2. UNESCO's rapid assessment mission report for 23–27 April 2016 records Palmyra under armed-group control from 21 May 2015 through 27 March 2016. The report says that from 27 March the city, World Heritage site, and museum were under military control and that there was no civilian presence other than Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums staff at the time of the mission.
3. The same UNESCO report lists further intentional damage, including the cella and surrounding columns of the Temple of Ba'al on 30 August 2015, the Triumphal Arch on 4 October 2015, and other damage. It also records temporary military camps and roads in the property/buffer zone and warns that military use can threaten archaeological remains.
4. TIME's 7 April 2016 essay describes Palmyra as recently recaptured from ISIS after roughly ten months and dates this image to 1 April 2016. The date therefore falls five days after the control change recorded in the UNESCO report.

The actor labels should remain source-qualified. UNESCO's formal rapid report says “armed groups” for the control interval and describes intentional destruction; TIME and the local/issuer caption use Islamic State, IS, or ISIS. “Syrian government and allied forces” is a description of the recapturing side in TIME and related contemporary reporting, not a claim that the image itself demonstrates the military operation.

### Access and mediation

- TIME reports that Meloni, based in Paris and often working from Beirut, obtained a visa, arrived in Damascus on 26 March, and received Syrian government permission to enter Palmyra. TIME says a small group of journalists entered early, left that evening, and Meloni spent the day shadowing government forces. A commander wanted the visit to commemorate fighters killed there. This is a contemporary publication's account of access and stated military framing.
- TIME also reports that Meloni observed smoke from the modern city while bomb-disposal teams detonated booby traps and mines, and that he encountered surviving structures alongside the ruined Temple of Bel. These details are sequence context for the assignment, not proof that a blast appears in this frame.
- AFP correspondent Maher Al-Mounes's first-person report, dated 1 April 2016, records that an army minder initially said the old city was mined and that entry was later allowed only on condition that journalists follow army directions so they would not detonate mines. The report describes demining, controlled explosions, and the remains of the Temple of Bel. AFP's account corroborates the dangerous and managed access environment but is not evidence that Meloni followed the identical route or received identical instructions.
- UNESCO's later rapid mission independently records military control, temporary military use, and continuing demining/inaccessibility. This supports a cautious description of the photograph as a controlled aftermath view.

Museum interpretation: the access conditions are part of the object's provenance of looking. The frame can be read as a government-permitted, post-recapture image in which a soldier's obscured gesture and the surviving/reduced monument coexist. That interpretation should not be converted into a claim that Meloni's image is propaganda, nor into a claim of neutral or unrestricted access.

## 4. Meloni's practice and project history

### Artist and institutional statements

- Magnum's accessible official store profile describes Meloni as an Italian photographer known for conflict and its consequences for people and landscapes. It says he adopts a historicist approach, using reflection on past and contemporary social, political, and cultural contexts to challenge simplified narratives about war.
- The official Magnum photographer profile is the requested primary institutional source, but its direct page returned HTTP 403 on 2026-08-08. Search indexing exposes the same profile's broad claims: Meloni was born in 1983, became a full Magnum member in 2020, has covered conflicts in Yemen, Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, and Lebanon, and his selected work “The Collapse of the Caliphate” charts the cross-border fight against Islamic State. The accessible Magnum store profile and GOST author biography corroborate the relevant biographical and practice claims. The exact current main-profile page remains unresolved.
- GOST Books' official page for _We Don't Say Goodbye_ describes a ten-year body of work in Iraq, Syria, and Libya, made from 2013–2019, covering the rise, reign, fall, and immediate aftermath of Islamic State as a territorial entity. The publisher presents Meloni's aim as understanding conflict through its roots, development, and historical context, with a fragmented sequence interrupted by objects and Islamic State writing found in war rubble. Meloni's statement on the page describes repetition and looping rather than a simple linear war narrative.
- GOST identifies the book as published in February 2022, 162 pages with 91 color images, ISBN `978-1-910401-44-6`. The book's project dates and form are established; inclusion of this exact Palmyra frame in the book is not established by the source checked.

### Exhibition and sequence leads

- Magnum's official profile links “The Collapse of the Caliphate” to its debut at Visa pour l'Image. The 2017 Visa press release identifies Meloni's exhibition, and an accessible copy of the exhibition brochure identifies a sequence built around the battles for Kobanî, Palmyra, Sirte, and Mosul, with the Palmyra recapture as one stage in a cross-border history of the Islamic State. This supports project-level context, not exact-frame inclusion.
- The official Magnum story “Kobane: The Aftermath” (2015) is a useful practice precedent: Meloni returned to document civilians rebuilding after destruction and discussed found photographic material in rubble. It supports a recurring interest in aftermath, fragments, memory, and cultural loss, but it does not establish a direct sequence relationship with archive number `MEL2016002G0401/4032`.
- TIME's 2016 photo essay is the strongest contemporary publication lead for this exact frame. TIME's “Best Photojournalism of 2016” list also includes the Meloni image/caption. The Guardian's 30 November 2022 gallery for Magnum 75 labels the work “Lorenzo Meloni: Palmyra, Syria, 2016” and describes the soldier seated on the former Temple of Bel's rubble. The Guardian is an independent caption corroboration, not the issuer of the archive number.
- A later Magnum/Chronicles history places Meloni's work in a broader visual history of ISIS and Palmyra. It is a useful contextual lead only; it does not establish that this tokenized frame appears in that publication.

## 10. Object essay notes (Museum interpretation)

This is a photograph of an aftermath pause. A soldier's face is hidden behind the helmet he lifts or removes, while a ruined monumental site occupies more visual space than the person. The gesture is neither legible as celebration nor grief from the image alone; its restraint keeps the frame between report, memorial, and controlled military tour.

The caption's identification of the former Temple of Bel gives the rubble a historical name, while UNESCO's chronology makes clear that the photograph follows the destruction by months and follows the recapture by five days. The image therefore should be written as a view after destruction and after a change of control—not as an image of the blast, the battle, or an unmediated “before/after.” The surviving columns matter precisely because they resist a totalizing claim that the ancient site was wholly erased.

Meloni's historicist and fragmentary project history makes this small gesture legible within a longer inquiry into war's repeated forms, political memory, ruins, and the aftermath of Islamic State. The military permission and escort context should remain visible in the essay: access made the picture possible, and it also shaped what could be seen, where the photographer could stand, and how the scene was narrated. That is a Museum interpretation grounded in TIME, AFP, UNESCO, Magnum, and GOST; it is not a claim that the image endorses or refutes any party's political account.

Recommended concise object note: “On 1 April 2016, five days after Syrian government and allied forces retook Palmyra from Islamic State control, Lorenzo Meloni photographed a helmeted Syrian army soldier seated among the rubble identified in the contemporary caption as the former Temple of Bel. The frame records neither the temple's 30 August 2015 destruction nor the recapture battle. Made under Syrian government permission and amid continuing mine clearance, it holds a private, obscured gesture against the surviving columns of a World Heritage site, linking Meloni's historicist study of war's aftermath to the political conditions under which the ruin could be photographed.”
