# 6529 Network Museum curatorial publication standard

- **Status:** active editorial and implementation standard
- **Applies to:** artist profiles and monographs, project and collection essays, artwork entries, acquisition and gift narratives, exhibition texts, labels, and public research features
- **Initial reference package:** Casey Reas accession `6529NM.2026.001`

## 1. The Museum publishes arguments about art

The 6529 Network Museum publishes writing that helps people see, experience, and understand works of art. Each text should make a specific work, practice, collection, or historical problem newly intelligible.

Accuracy, citation, and review are prerequisites. Museum publication must also advance an argument, look closely, distinguish media, establish historical relations, and support its judgments.

Curatorial writing is one of the Museum's primary cultural forms. The collection becomes publicly meaningful through the relation among artworks, display, scholarship, provenance, conservation, and encounter.

## 2. What every publication must achieve

Every publication must do five things:

1. **Name its subject precisely.** Identify the artist, work, project, event, and time period actually under discussion.
2. **Advance a thesis.** Make a claim that organizes the text and could be debated, refined, or rejected.
3. **Describe works.** Give readers sustained, exact encounters with form, behavior, material, scale, time, and display.
4. **Build context.** Explain historical genealogies, intellectual problems, production conditions, and reception without reducing the work to them.
5. **Return to the collection.** Show why the Museum's exact object or group matters and what it enables the public to understand.

If a text could be attached unchanged to another artist by replacing names and titles, it fails.

## 3. Evidence supports the argument

The Museum's records distinguish chain evidence, artist statements, platform records, Museum observations, and interpretation. Public scholarship preserves those distinctions while giving the artwork and the argument priority.

The body of an essay should be fluent and direct. Notes carry most factual attribution. A caveat belongs in the body only when it changes the meaning of the work—for example, when a still image cannot represent a continuous software performance, when an unresolved date changes chronology, or when a work widely described as autonomous depends on a mutable service.

Avoid prose such as:

- “This is a Museum interpretation and not a claim about artist intent” after every interpretive sentence;
- “The source does not establish value, quality, or title” when none of those claims is being made;
- long lists of what a document does not prove;
- descriptions organized one source at a time;
- technical qualifications inserted before the reader has encountered the artwork.

Use notes, source registers, rights statements, condition reports, and provenance records for those functions. The essay should synthesize their consequences.

## 4. Research before prose

Research begins with questions, not a search-result inventory.

For an artist, establish:

- intellectual and artistic formation;
- changes in medium, method, subject, and display;
- major bodies of work and their chronology;
- recurring problems and meaningful breaks;
- the artist's relation to predecessors and contemporaries;
- teaching, writing, tool-building, publishing, organizing, or institution-building where those activities alter the art;
- exhibitions, collections, publications, and critical reception;
- recent work through a stated research cutoff;
- the exact relation of the Museum's holdings to the larger practice.

For a project or series, establish:

- genesis and date range;
- conceptual and material problem;
- constitutive system, process, or technique;
- relation among individual works and the project as a whole;
- edition or variation logic where applicable;
- modes of display and audience encounter;
- exhibition, distribution, and publication history;
- relation to earlier and later work;
- critical reception and unresolved questions;
- the role of the Museum's exact object.

For an object, establish:

- exact identity and title history;
- medium as experienced and constituted, not merely token standard;
- scale, duration, behavior, interaction, sound, orientation, and environment;
- the difference among live work, artist-authorized manifestation, Museum display, and documentation surrogate;
- project position and object-specific variation;
- acquisition, provenance, rights, condition, and preservation facts needed for public understanding;
- a close-looking record made from the exact work.

## 5. Source hierarchy

Use sources in this order, while recognizing that different sources answer different questions.

### 5.1 Primary and official sources

- the artwork and its constitutive materials;
- artist-controlled archives, writings, interviews, lectures, books, and technical notes;
- commissioning and collecting institutions;
- galleries, publishers, and presenting platforms for facts within their authority;
- contracts, token metadata, scripts, chain records, and official technical documentation;
- the Museum's retained accession, observation, condition, rights, provenance, and preservation evidence.

Primary sources establish what an artist, institution, or system records. They do not remove the need for critical analysis.

### 5.2 Scholarship and criticism

Use serious art history, exhibition catalogues, peer-reviewed scholarship, conservation research, and criticism to place the work under interpretive pressure. Prefer sources that engage the actual work, not broad trend pieces in which the artist appears only as an example.

Criticism need not be favorable. A perceptive negative or ambivalent review may disclose scale, fatigue, discomfort, illegibility, or failed integration that promotional writing omits.

### 5.3 Contextual reporting

Reputable reporting can document reception, events, and contemporary language. It should not be the sole source for object identity, chronology, artist intent, or technical constitution.

Marketplace listings, rarity sites, auction promotion, social posts without corroboration, unattributed collector claims, and generic generated biographies are not scholarly authority.

## 6. Close looking for born-digital and tokenized art

Close looking is not limited to a still image. For software, generative, interactive, moving-image, sound, and networked works, it includes behavior over time and the conditions under which that behavior appears.

An object observation should record:

- initial visual or sonic encounter;
- dominant and subordinate forms;
- palette, contrast, density, rhythm, direction, edge, interval, figure/field, depth, and scale;
- how attention moves through the work;
- what changes, repeats, accumulates, pauses, recomposes, or responds;
- the relation between local events and the total field;
- the effect of duration and interaction;
- the role of screen, projection, browser, speakers, physical support, and surrounding space;
- differences between a retained still and the live manifestation.

The observer must identify the exact token or edition state. Project-level language cannot substitute for object-level looking. Traits may clarify construction but may not become automatic interpretations. “Palette A,” “Janky,” “Growth 4,” or a low-frequency feature is not an aesthetic judgment.

Good description is selective. It does not inventory every visible element. It identifies the formal relations that drive the work and uses those relations to support the essay's argument.

## 7. Historical genealogy without name-dropping

A genealogy must state the operation that connects two practices and the difference introduced by the later work.

Weak:

> The work recalls Sol LeWitt, Ellsworth Kelly, and Victor Vasarely.

Stronger:

> The work takes Kelly's cutting and reassembly as an operation rather than a style, then places that operation inside a continuously executable system whose states can be distributed across an edition.

For every historical relation, ask:

- Is influence documented, inferred, or simply an affinity?
- What specific work, method, text, or exhibition makes the relation useful?
- What changes when the operation moves into this medium?
- What does the comparison reveal that close looking alone would not?
- Does the comparison flatten meaningful differences in politics, institution, labor, or technology?

The Museum should not construct a prestige genealogy merely to elevate a digital work. History is an analytical tool, not a credential.

## 8. Medium must be described at the level of the artwork

“NFT,” “digital image,” and “generative art” are usually insufficient media descriptions.

A complete account may need to distinguish:

- natural-language instruction;
- source code and language;
- dependency and runtime;
- token hash, seed, or parameter state;
- browser and graphics pipeline;
- live screen or projection performance;
- interaction and duration;
- static, video, or audio documentation;
- print, plotter drawing, book, sculpture, or installation counterpart;
- contract and token as identity, edition, provenance, and transfer infrastructure.

The text must explain which of these layers are constitutive, which are display conditions, which are records, and which are related manifestations. It should not reduce the work to code, a token, or a screenshot.

## 9. Forms of publication

### 9.1 Artist monograph or major profile

A substantial artist text needs:

- a governing thesis introduced through a work or problem, not a résumé;
- intellectual biography;
- chronological development across every major relevant phase;
- art-historical genealogy;
- repeated close readings;
- material and technical development;
- exhibition and reception history;
- recent work through the research cutoff;
- the Museum collection in context;
- notes, chronology, and bibliography.

For a major mature practice, 12,000-20,000 words may be appropriate. Length must follow the argument; it is not an acceptance criterion by itself.

### 9.2 Project or series essay

A project essay should normally contain:

- genesis;
- formal and conceptual system;
- material constitution;
- variation or edition logic;
- close readings of several states;
- display and distribution history;
- relation to the larger practice;
- reception;
- the Museum object's position.

The project is not merely context for a token. In long-form generative art, the project often defines the possibility space within which each token is one realization.

### 9.3 Collection or accession essay

A collection essay must explain why exact objects belong together. It should state:

- the group's organizing claim;
- what each object contributes;
- where comparison is productive;
- what the group omits;
- whether the grouping is artist-defined, donor-formed, curator-formed, or historically inherited;
- how the group changes the Museum's collection.

Do not call a donor- or Museum-formed group a triptych, suite, retrospective, or canonical selection without evidence.

### 9.4 Object entry

Every major object receives two layers:

- a concise label of approximately 100-200 words;
- an extended entry, often 800-1,500 words for a significant born-digital work.

The extended entry should include first encounter, formal analysis, temporal behavior, project relation, medium, historical context, and collection role. Identity, provenance, rights, and preservation should be available beside it in designed record views, not repeated as boilerplate inside every paragraph.

### 9.5 Gift or acquisition narrative

This form interprets an institutional act. It is distinct from a gift agreement, accession certificate, donor acknowledgment, or provenance record.

It should explain:

- what entered the collection;
- the artistic and historical significance of the exact group;
- what the acquisition makes publicly possible;
- what is documented about donor formation or selection;
- what is not known about donor motive;
- why acceptance serves the Museum's mission;
- what obligations acceptance creates.

The significance of a gift rests on the art and on what its acceptance makes possible for the public.

## 10. Writing about artists as builders of tools and institutions

Tools, schools, foundations, platforms, and communities belong in an artist's profile when they clarify the practice's conception of authorship, access, labor, circulation, or preservation.

Avoid the résumé form:

> The artist co-founded X, taught at Y, and exhibited at Z.

Ask instead:

- Does the tool embody the same formal or ethical commitments as the art?
- Does teaching change how the artist makes systems legible?
- Does a platform alter exhibition, ownership, peer exchange, or preservation?
- Is this institution-building itself part of the practice, or merely adjacent professional activity?

Processing and Feral File belong to distinct parts of Casey Reas's practice. Together they show how he has extended systems thinking beyond individual artworks into tools, pedagogy, exhibition, and circulation.

## 11. Technical and preservation writing in its proper place

Technical constitution can be aesthetically and historically significant. Browser behavior, code migration, dependency, emulation, and display are not clerical details for software art. But they should enter a curatorial text where they illuminate material or meaning.

Examples:

- a code migration can reveal which behaviors the artist considered essential;
- a continuous non-repeating work changes what a still image can claim;
- a dependency on an obsolete runtime may become part of exhibition history;
- an on-chain script may alter recovery and distribution without removing the need for a browser;
- a plotter drawing may disclose structure that motion obscures.

Detailed hashes, capture timestamps, viewport geometry, compliance screening, and release commitments belong in technical and registrar records unless the argument directly depends on them.

## 12. Editorial voice

The Museum's voice should be informed, exact, lucid, and willing to make judgments.

Prefer:

- concrete nouns and active verbs;
- paragraphs organized around one developing claim;
- visual specificity;
- transitions that show conceptual movement;
- precise qualification where evidence requires it;
- a rhythm that allows both sustained argument and moments of looking.

Avoid:

- “explores the intersection of” as a substitute for an argument;
- “challenges the boundaries of” without naming a boundary and consequence;
- “invites the viewer” when the work does something more exact;
- “innovative,” “groundbreaking,” “seminal,” or “important” without demonstration;
- promotional superlatives;
- generic claims about technology changing society;
- paragraphs that restate titles and metadata;
- repetitive “not X but Y” formulas;
- process announcements about future reviewers, governance, or planned research.

The reader should feel the writer has spent time with the work and has something at stake in the interpretation.

## 13. Notes, bibliography, and research boundary

Every long-form publication includes:

- a version and publication date;
- author or institutional authorship;
- a research cutoff date;
- numbered notes supporting facts and direct quotations;
- a selected bibliography divided where useful into primary sources, scholarship, criticism, and technical records;
- a link to the applicable Museum source and accession records;
- a revision history.

Sources should be cited near the claim they support. A bibliography cannot rescue unsourced prose. Links must point directly to the relevant page or retained source, not a search result.

## 14. Revision and supersession

Publication scholarship evolves. Corrections and new interpretations should be visible.

- factual correction: issue a new version with a dated correction note and exact supersession target;
- expanded scholarship: publish a new version and preserve the prior text in Git history or an explicit archive;
- changed interpretation: state what changed and why when the shift is material;
- altered collection status: update the controlling record first, then revise public prose;
- rights or display change: update the rights/condition record and all affected presentations.

Do not silently rewrite a historical accession assertion. Do not preserve a known public error merely because an earlier release was reviewed.

## 15. Acceptance test

Before publication, answer yes to all of the following:

- Can the thesis be stated without repeating the title?
- Does the text describe exact works at meaningful length?
- Are historical comparisons operational and specific?
- Are biography and institutions connected to artistic questions?
- Are medium and manifestation distinguished correctly?
- Does the writing include recent work and reception?
- Are facts sourced and interpretations argued?
- Has generic or promotional language been removed?
- Does the exact Museum holding matter to the text?
- Are technical and registrar details proportionate?
- Does the prose read as finished publication, not a plan for someone else to finish?

If any answer is no, the text is not ready.
